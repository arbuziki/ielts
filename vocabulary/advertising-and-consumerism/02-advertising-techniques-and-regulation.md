## Vocabulary

### Key terms
| Chunk | Meaning | Example | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| a misleading claim | a statement that gives a false impression | The company should never have made **a misleading claim** about its vitamins; the scandal has cost it thousands of customers. | C · neutral · verb: *make a claim* · Grammar: modal perfect |
| false advertising | advertising that contains untrue information | Were regulators to fine every brand that engages in **false advertising**, misleading campaigns would soon become too costly to run. | U · neutral · Grammar: inversion conditional |
| a celebrity endorsement | a famous person publicly supporting a product | It is **a celebrity endorsement**, rather than any real improvement in quality, that makes an ordinary perfume seem glamorous. | C · neutral · verb: *endorse a product* · Grammar: cleft sentence |
| targeted advertising | ads chosen for you using your online data | **Targeted advertising** relies on a system whereby every click is recorded and used to build a profile of the user. | U · neutral · Grammar: relative: whose / whereby / in which |
| sponsored content | posts or articles paid for by a brand | **Sponsored content** must be clearly marked so that readers can tell the difference between advertising and journalism. | U · neutral · NOT *a sponsored content* · Grammar: modal passive |
| native advertising | ads designed to look like normal articles or posts | Designed to look like ordinary news stories, **native advertising** blurs the line between journalism and marketing. | U · neutral · Grammar: participle clause |

| consumer protection | laws and rules that safeguard buyers | If stronger **consumer protection** had been introduced years ago, online shoppers would now feel far safer buying from unknown sellers. | U · neutral · *consumer protection laws* · Grammar: mixed conditional |

## Text 1 — Discuss both views and give your opinion
> Advertisements often exaggerate the benefits of the products they promote. Some people think this is harmless, while others believe advertising should be controlled more strictly. Discuss both views and give your own opinion.

Some people regard exaggeration as a harmless part of advertising. They point out that nobody believes a **slogan** such as "the best coffee in the world" literally, and that most consumers **take** such boasts **with a grain of salt**. **Given that** shoppers can now compare prices and read reviews online, they argue, there is no real harm in an ad that **plays up** a product's qualities. However, I share the view of those who want stricter control, because exaggeration easily turns into **misleading claims** about health, safety or the environment. A notorious case is Volkswagen's "clean diesel" campaign, which collapsed in 2015 when it emerged that the company had installed software to cheat emissions tests. Hundreds of thousands of drivers had been **taken in** by what was, in fact, **greenwashing**. Stricter rules would allow **an advertising watchdog** to demand that every factual claim be **backed up** with evidence, and to **impose fines** on firms that **breach advertising standards**. Companies could still use humor and **celebrity endorsements**, **provided that** their factual statements are accurate. Similarly, **sponsored content** could be allowed **on condition that** it is **clearly labeled**. **In the event of** a complaint being **upheld**, the ad should be **withdrawn** at once. Such rules are not designed to limit creativity; they are introduced **with a view to** protecting people who buy products **in good faith**.

**Notes**
- **Topic vocabulary:** *misleading claims*, *greenwashing*, *sponsored content*, *celebrity endorsements* — four types of problem advertising; *greenwashing* and *sponsored content* are uncountable.
- **Topic vocabulary:** *an advertising watchdog* — the neutral, journalistic word for a regulator; reusable for any industry (*a media / financial watchdog*).
- **Collocations:** *impose fines on sb*, *breach advertising standards*, *uphold a complaint*, *withdraw an ad* — the language of regulation; all formal and precise.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *play sth up* (*an ad that plays up a product's qualities*), *be taken in by sth*, *back sth up with evidence*.
- **Fixed expressions:** *take sth with a grain of salt* (the "harmless" side; BrE: *with a pinch of salt*), *in good faith* (the consumer's side).
- **Frames:** *Given that + clause, …* presents an accepted fact as a reason; *provided that* and *on condition that* both mean "only if" — *on condition that* is more formal.
- **Frames:** *In the event of + noun / -ing* (= if sth happens); *with a view to + -ing* (= with the aim of) — note the *-ing*: NOT *with a view to protect*.
- **Specific example:** the Volkswagen emissions scandal (2015) — a well-known, accurate case of advertising that misled consumers.
- **AmE spelling:** *labeled*, *humor* (BrE: *labelled*, *humour*).

## Text 2 — Letter (formal)
> You recently bought a product that did not do what its advertisement promised. Write a letter to the company. In your letter: explain what you bought and why; describe how it failed to match the ad; say what you want the company to do.

Dear Sir or Madam,

I am writing to complain about a VacuMax 300 cordless vacuum cleaner, which I ordered from your website on September 3 (order number 48215).

I chose this model because your TV commercial stated that it would run for 60 minutes on a single charge and remove pet hair from any carpet. As I have two cats and a son with an allergy, I was willing to pay more **for the sake of** my family's health.

Unfortunately, the product **falls** far **short of** these promises. **As far as** battery life **is concerned**, it lasts barely 20 minutes, and **when it comes to** pet hair, it leaves most of it behind. According to **the fine print**, the 60-minute figure applies only to the lowest setting. The ad therefore gives **a false impression** and is likely to **mislead consumers**. Sales appear to have been boosted **at the expense of** honesty.

**In terms of** resolving this matter, I would like to return the item and **request a full refund**, to be paid within 14 days. I would also ask you to **look into** the wording of the ad.

I look forward to your prompt reply.

Sincerely,
Anna Petrova

**Notes**
- **Collocations:** *give a false impression*, *mislead consumers*, *request a full refund* — formal, exact phrases for any complaint letter.
- **Fixed expression:** *fall (far) short of promises / expectations* — *far* intensifies it; more precise than "was bad".
- **Fixed expression:** *according to the fine print* — always *the fine print* (BrE: *the small print*); useful for any contract or offer.
- **Phrasal verb:** *look into sth* (= investigate) — a polite, formal request to a company.
- **Frames:** *As far as + noun + is concerned, …* and *When it comes to + noun, …* — two ways to move between aspects of a problem; use one each, not the same one twice.
- **Frames:** *In terms of + noun / -ing, …* (*In terms of resolving this matter, …*) — a neat formal way to start the "what I want" paragraph.
- **Frames:** *for the sake of + noun* (= to benefit); *X at the expense of Y* (= gaining X by harming Y) — both reusable in essays (*growth at the expense of the environment*).
- **Register:** no contractions; *Dear Sir or Madam,* → *Sincerely,* (BrE: *Yours faithfully*). AmE date: *September 3* (BrE: *3 September*).
