## Text 1 — Opinion essay
> Some people think that all advertising aimed at children should be banned. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

I agree **for the most part** that advertising aimed at children should be banned, although a total ban would be difficult to enforce online. Young children are **at an impressionable age**: they **take** ads **at face value** and cannot yet **see through** the **persuasive techniques** that adults recognize. **In other words**, advertisers are not competing fairly but **exploiting children's trust**. **More often than not**, the products being promoted are toys or **junk food**, and a child who has been **bombarded with** cartoon-style ads for sugary cereal soon **talks** his parents **into** buying it. Many parents **give in to** this **pester power** simply to avoid an argument, which can lead to **unhealthy eating habits** and, in the long run, **childhood obesity**. Critics call such a ban **the nanny state** at work, but there is evidence that it can succeed. The Canadian province of Quebec has banned commercial advertising directed at children under 13 since 1980, and research suggests that the rule has reduced fast-food spending among families there. **In principle**, the same approach could be extended to the internet, where **child influencers** and **in-app advertising** reach **a captive audience** on tablets; **in practice**, this would require regulators to **crack down on** platforms that **cash in on** young viewers. Even so, I believe a clear legal ban is the most effective way to protect children.

**Notes**
- **Topic vocabulary:** *pester power*, *junk food*, *childhood obesity* — the three core nouns for this topic; all uncountable, so no *a* and no plural.
- **Topic vocabulary:** *child influencers*, *in-app advertising* — show you know that advertising has moved online; reusable in any technology essay.
- **Collocations:** *exploit children's trust*, *be bombarded with ads*, *persuasive techniques*, *unhealthy eating habits* — exact verb–noun and adjective–noun pairs; learn them as blocks.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *talk sb into doing sth* (object in the middle), *give in to sth*, *see through sth*, *crack down on sth*, *cash in on sth*.
- **Fixed expressions:** *at an impressionable age*, *take sth at face value*, *a captive audience*, *the nanny state* (always with *the*).
- **Frames:** *For the most part* softens your agreement (= mostly); *In other words, …* restates the previous point more simply; *More often than not, …* = usually.
- **Frame:** *In principle, X could …; in practice, this would require …* — a ready contrast between theory and reality for any policy question (taxes, bans, quotas).
- **Specific example:** Quebec's ban on advertising to children under 13 (since 1980) is a real, checkable fact; a named place and a date make the argument convincing.
- **AmE:** *ad / ads* (BrE often: *advert / adverts*); *recognize* (BrE: *recognise*).

## Text 2 — Essay paragraph — A total ban is unrealistic; teaching children to think critically protects them better
> Some people think that all advertising aimed at children should be banned. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

A total ban, however, is unrealistic, and teaching children to question ads would offer better protection. **Owing to** the spread of smartphones, children now spend hours on video platforms, where marketing is blended with entertainment through **product placement** and sponsored videos. Even **a blanket ban** would, **in effect**, leave this online world largely untouched, because most platforms are based abroad and are extremely difficult to **rein in**. **In view of** this, the most realistic defense is to train children to think critically **from an early age**. Parents can **lead by example** by talking about ads at home and explaining why a toy looks bigger on screen than in the store. Schools, too, can include **media literacy** in the curriculum, so that students learn to **distinguish between** advertising and genuine content and to **pick up on** hidden messages. **As a consequence**, children become less likely to **fall for** exaggerated claims. An untrained seven-year-old **is no match for** a professional marketing team; a child who has learned the tricks, by contrast, can recognize them. **For this reason**, education, rather than prohibition, is the more lasting solution: most children **grow out of** a toy within weeks, but the ability to question an ad lasts a lifetime.

**Notes**
- **Topic vocabulary:** *product placement*, *media literacy* — both uncountable; *media literacy* is the key term for the "education" side of this debate.
- **Collocations:** *a blanket ban* (= a ban with no exceptions), *distinguish between X and Y*, *include sth in the curriculum*.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *rein sb/sth in* (control), *pick up on sth* (notice), *fall for sth* (be tricked), *grow out of sth* (become too old for).
- **Fixed expressions:** *from an early age* (remember the article), *lead by example*, *be no match for sb* — useful whenever you compare a weak side with a strong one.
- **Frames:** *Owing to + noun* (formal "because of"); *In view of this, …* introduces the conclusion you draw from a fact.
- **Frame:** *X would, in effect, …* = what really happens in practice; good for showing that a policy has a hidden weakness.
- **Frames:** *As a consequence, …* for a result; *For this reason, …* to lead into your final judgment — two different linkers, so you do not repeat *therefore*.
- **AmE:** *defense* (BrE: *defence*), *store* (BrE: *shop*), *students* (BrE often: *pupils*), *judgment* (BrE also: *judgement*).
