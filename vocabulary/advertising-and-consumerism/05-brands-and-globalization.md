## Text 1 — Problem and solution
> International brands and chains are spreading all over the world, and as a result many small local businesses are closing. What problems does this cause, and how can these problems be solved?

The spread of **global chains** is **a double-edged sword**: it brings convenience, but it also causes serious problems. **Above all**, it threatens the character of local communities. When the same coffee shops and fashion **franchises** appear on every main street, towns **lose their unique character** and become **cookie-cutter** places – what the British think tank the New Economics Foundation called "clone towns" in a 2004 report. **Take** family-run cafés, **for example**. Unable to match the prices of **multinationals** that benefit from **economies of scale**, many are **squeezed out** within a few years. This creates **a vicious cycle**: as independent businesses close, profits flow to distant corporate headquarters instead of staying in the area, fewer local jobs are created, and even more small firms struggle. Over time, **cultural identity** is **eroded** as **local produce** and traditional crafts disappear from the stores. **The root cause of** the problem lies in the absence of **a level playing field**, since large corporations can **undercut competitors** and often negotiate lower rents. Governments could tackle this by cutting business taxes for independent stores and using zoning laws to limit the number of chain outlets in historic districts. Consumers also have a part to play: by choosing to **buy local** and **support local businesses**, they can keep their towns distinctive.

**Notes**
- **Topic vocabulary:** *global chains*, *franchises*, *multinationals*, *cookie-cutter* places — four precise words instead of repeating "big companies" or "the same".
- **Topic vocabulary:** *economies of scale* (always plural), *local produce* (uncountable, no *-s*), *cultural identity*.
- **Collocations:** *lose their unique character*, *erode cultural identity* (here passive: *is eroded*), *undercut competitors*, *buy local*, *support local businesses*.
- **Phrasal verb:** *squeeze sb out* — very often passive: *small firms are squeezed out by …*.
- **Idiom:** *a level playing field* — perfect for any fairness argument (trade, education, sports).
- **Frames:** *X is a double-edged sword: it …, but it also …* — a clean opening that shows both sides; *Above all, …* introduces the most important problem.
- **Frames:** *Take + noun, for example.* (a short sentence on its own) and *This creates a vicious cycle: …* — explain each step of the cycle after the colon (BrE also: *a vicious circle*).
- **Frame:** *The root cause of the problem lies in + noun* — leads naturally from problems to solutions.
- **Specific example:** the "clone town" idea from the New Economics Foundation's 2004 report — real and easy to remember; *cookie-cutter* is the everyday AmE way to say the same thing.
- **AmE:** *zoning laws* (BrE: *planning laws*), *corporate headquarters* (BrE often: *head office*), *main street* (BrE: *high street*).

## Text 2 — Essay paragraph — Globalization has given consumers higher standards, lower prices and more choice
> International brands and chains are spreading all over the world. Do the advantages of this development outweigh the disadvantages?

On the other hand, it would be wrong to ignore what globalization has given consumers. International brands **drive down** prices through **mass production** and efficient supply chains, while **fierce competition** between them has **widened consumer choice** and **raised quality standards**. When McDonald's opened its first restaurant in Moscow in 1990, for instance, it introduced standards of hygiene and service that were new to many Soviet customers, and it worked with local farmers to improve their produce. This **paved the way for** other foreign firms to arrive, and many Russian cafés and restaurants soon **followed suit**, adopting similar standards. **It is also important to bear in mind that** competition does not always **wipe out** small companies. Those that **stand out** by offering something distinctive, such as regional dishes or handmade goods, often survive and even **branch out** into new markets, sometimes becoming **household names** themselves. In this sense, global brands can **play a key role in** pushing local firms to improve. Of course, governments need to **take into account** the impact on jobs and traditions, and the aim should be to **strike a balance between** the efficiency of global companies and the variety that local businesses provide. On balance, however, the advantages outweigh the drawbacks: without international brands, ordinary people would pay more for less choice.

**Notes**
- **Topic vocabulary:** *mass production*, *household names* — the positive side of globalization in two chunks.
- **Collocations:** *fierce competition*, *widen consumer choice*, *raise quality standards* (NOT *rise*) — the three benefits named in the heading.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *drive sth down* (prices), *wipe sth out*, *stand out*, *branch out into sth*.
- **Idiom:** *follow suit* — no article: NOT *follow the suit*.
- **Frames:** *X paved the way for Y* (made Y possible); *play a key role in + noun / -ing* — both work in almost any topic (technology, education, health).
- **Frames:** *It is important to bear in mind that + clause* — introduces a point the reader might forget; *take into account + long noun phrase* (put the long object after *account*).
- **Frame:** *strike a balance between X and Y* — the classic way to end a paragraph that weighs two sides.
- **Specific example:** McDonald's first Moscow restaurant (1990) — a famous, accurate example of a global brand bringing new standards.
- **AmE spelling:** *globalization* (BrE also: *globalisation*).
