## Vocabulary

| Chunk | Meaning | Example | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| social media | websites and apps for sharing content and contacts | Most teenagers use **social media** every day. | U · neutral · NOT *a social media* → a social media platform |
| a social media platform | one particular site or app, e.g. Instagram | TikTok is **a social media platform** built around short videos. | C · neutral · also: a messaging app |

## Text 1 — Opinion essay
> Some people believe that social media has made people less sociable and more isolated. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

It is often claimed that **social media** has made people less sociable. While I accept that heavy use can lead to **social isolation**, I would argue that the problem lies in how these platforms are used rather than in the technology itself.

**At the heart of** the problem **is** passive use. Teenagers who **scroll through** their **news feeds** for hours inevitably **compare themselves with** their peers, yet what they see is **a highlight reel** rather than real life. This can damage their **self-esteem**, which **has a ripple effect on** their offline lives: young people who feel inadequate are less likely to **strike up conversations** or **open up to** friends.

However, **loneliness** existed **on a large scale** long before the internet. When Britain appointed its first Minister for Loneliness in 2018, much of the public discussion focused on elderly people living alone, not on teenagers with smartphones. For students and migrants abroad, meanwhile, messaging apps make it far easier to **keep in touch with** family and to **catch up with** old friends from whom they might otherwise have **drifted apart**.

I therefore agree with the statement only to a limited extent. Rather than reacting once people already **feel isolated**, we should prevent online chat from replacing **face-to-face contact** **in the first place**. **To this end**, schools could teach children to use apps to arrange meetings rather than to avoid them.

**Notes**
- **Topic vocabulary:** *social isolation*, *loneliness*, *self-esteem* — all U, so no *a* and no plural; *a highlight reel* — a vivid but neutral way to describe idealized profiles.
- **Collocations:** *compare yourself with others*, *strike up a conversation*, *feel isolated* — verb + noun/adjective pairs that fit any paragraph on well-being.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *scroll through* + feed / photos; *open up to* + sb (*about* sth); *catch up with* + sb; *drift apart from* + sb (or plural subject + *drift apart*).
- **Fixed expressions & frames:** *keep in touch with* + sb — the standard phrase for long-distance contact.
- *At the heart of the problem is* + noun — sounds analytical; reuse: "At the heart of the problem is a lack of affordable housing."
- *X has a ripple effect on Y* — for chains of cause and effect in any topic (transportation, the economy, health). BrE: *a knock-on effect*.
- *on a large scale* — reuse: "Plastic waste is produced on a large scale."
- *prevent X from + -ing in the first place* — stresses prevention rather than cure; works for crime, health and pollution.
- *To this end,* + measure — a formal link from a goal to a concrete step; keep it for essays.

## Text 2 — Letter (informal)
> You have lost touch with an old friend, and you only see each other's posts on social media. Write a letter to your friend. In your letter, say why you are writing, tell them some news about your life, and suggest meeting up in person.

Hi Tom,

I was **scrolling through** Instagram last night and saw your photos from the lake, and it suddenly hit me that we haven't actually talked in nearly two years! **Liking** each other's **posts** isn't really the same as **keeping in touch**, is it? I'd hate for us to **drift apart** just because we're both busy.

So, my news: I moved to Chicago in the spring for a new job. I **get along** really well **with** my team, although I've **had a falling-out with** my roommate over the dishes, so I'm looking for a new place! The commute is tiring, but **on balance** I'm glad I made the move.

Anyway, why don't we **meet up** for real? I'm back home the last weekend of May, so we could grab a coffee or just **chill out** at your place like we used to. And I promise I won't **show up** an hour late this time! It'd be so good to **catch up** **face to face** for a change.

Let me know what works for you.

Take care,
Anna

**Notes**
- **Topic vocabulary:** *face to face* — adverb, no hyphens (compare the adjective in *face-to-face contact*).
- **Collocations:** *like a post* — the natural verb for social media reactions; *liking each other's posts* is a handy contrast with real contact.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *drift apart*, *meet up*, *catch up* — the core "friendship" set for any informal letter.
- *get along (well) with* + sb — coworkers, neighbors, roommates; *really well* goes between *get along* and *with*. BrE: *get on (well) with*.
- *have a falling-out with* + sb + *over* + noun — the quarrel and its cause in one phrase. BrE: *fall out with* sb.
- *show up* (late / an hour late) — = arrive; ideal for jokes and apologies in informal letters. Also: *turn up* (more BrE).
- *chill out* — informal only; in an essay use *relax*.
- **Fixed expressions & frames:** *keep in touch*; *On balance,* + judgment — weighs pros and cons in a few words and also works in essays.
- Register: *Hi Tom,* … *Take care,* — a friendly opening and closing; contractions (*I'd*, *it'd*, *won't*), a question tag (*is it?*) and an exclamation point keep the tone friendly.
