# News & Misinformation
*Media & the Internet · news from social media vs traditional media, fake news and misinformation, clickbait, echo chambers, fact-checking, trust in journalists, the decline of print newspapers*

## Text 1 — Advantages-disadvantages essay
> Many people now get their news from social media rather than from newspapers or television. Do the advantages of this development outweigh the disadvantages?

**A growing number of** people now **turn to** social media rather than newspapers or television for their news. Although this trend carries real risks, **I am inclined to believe that** **the benefits of** this shift **outweigh the drawbacks**, provided that readers stay critical.

The main advantages are speed and diversity. **Breaking news** appears online **in real time**, and anyone with a smartphone can report events that professional journalists might miss. During the Arab Spring in 2011, for example, Egyptian protesters shared footage of the demonstrations on Facebook and Twitter while state television **played** them **down**. Such **citizen journalism** gives a voice to people whom **the mainstream media** often ignores.

On the other hand, **the vast majority of** posts are never checked by an editor, so **fake news** and **clickbait** can **go viral** before anyone has time to **verify the facts**. **Algorithms** also show users more of what they already agree with, trapping them in **echo chambers** where opposing views rarely appear.

These dangers are serious, but they can be reduced, whereas the value of open access to information is hard to replace. **There is a strong case for** teaching **media literacy** in schools, so that young people learn to check whether a story comes from **a reliable source** and to **take** shocking claims **with a grain of salt**.

**Notes**
- **Topic vocabulary:** *citizen journalism*, *fake news*, *clickbait*, *media literacy* — all U: no *a*, no plural -s; *the mainstream media* always takes *the*.
- **Collocations:** *breaking news* + *in real time*; *verify the facts*; *a reliable source* — the "checking" set for any media question.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *turn to* + source (+ *for* + thing); *play sth down* — a pronoun goes in the middle: *played them down*.
- **Idioms:** *go viral* (NOT *become viral*); *take sth with a grain of salt* (BrE: *with a pinch of salt*).
- *A growing number of* + plural noun + plural verb — a neutral trend opener for any topic.
- *I am inclined to believe that* + clause — a measured way to give your view when you will also make concessions.
- *the benefits of X outweigh the drawbacks* — the core answer frame for advantages/disadvantages questions.
- *the vast majority of* + plural noun — signals "most" without inventing a statistic.
- *There is a strong case for* + noun / -ing — introduces a recommendation in a conclusion.

## Text 2 — Essay paragraph — who should stop false information?
> Who should be responsible for stopping the spread of false information online: individual readers or the technology companies that run the platforms?

Some argue that it is up to readers to **check the facts** before sharing a story, and I agree with this **up to a point**: nobody should **take** a shocking headline **at face value**. However, placing the burden on individuals alone is unrealistic, because platforms earn money from any post that **goes viral**, whether it is true or not. Their **algorithms** reward outrage **as opposed to** accuracy, so **misinformation** can reach millions of users before a **fact-checker** has had time to **look into** it. The consequences can be serious. **In the wake of** the COVID-19 outbreak, false claims linking 5G cell towers to the virus **spread** rapidly online, and in 2020 dozens of towers in Britain were set on fire. A system in which ordinary users must **weed out** such lies by themselves is **a far cry from** the strict rules that newspapers and broadcasters face: they can be fined or sued for publishing false claims. Tech companies should therefore be **held accountable** in the same way and legally required to **take down** harmful posts quickly. Governments must introduce such rules even **in the face of** strong opposition from the industry, because this is a problem that readers cannot solve on their own.

**Notes**
- **Topic vocabulary:** *misinformation* (U; countable form: *a piece of misinformation*); *a fact-checker* (C).
- **Collocations:** *hold sb accountable* — passive *be held accountable* is the usual essay form; *check the facts*; *spread* + misinformation / claims / rumors.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *look into* sth → *weed sth out* → *take sth down* — a complete "moderation" sequence you can reuse.
- **Idioms:** *take sth at face value* — the natural partner of "critical reading" arguments.
- *agree up to a point* — concede before you disagree; reuse in any discussion essay.
- *X as opposed to Y* — contrasts two nouns: "quantity as opposed to quality".
- *In the wake of* + event — formal "after and because of"; reuse: "in the wake of the financial crisis".
- *X is a far cry from Y* — stresses a big difference between two situations.
- *in the face of* + opposition / difficulty — "while having to deal with"; strong in recommendations.
- AmE: *a cell tower* (BrE: *a phone mast*).
