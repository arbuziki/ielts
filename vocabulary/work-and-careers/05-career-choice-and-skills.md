# Career Choice & Skills
*Work & Careers · choosing a career for money or passion, college vs vocational training, transferable skills, changing careers, lifelong learning, parental pressure on career choices*

## Vocabulary

### Key terms
| Chunk | Meaning | Example | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| transferable skills | skills that are useful in many different jobs | Were schools to focus more on **transferable skills**, graduates would adapt more easily when industries change. | plural · neutral · NOT *transferring skills* · Grammar: inversion conditional |
| soft skills | personal qualities such as communication and teamwork | Many graduates are said to lack **soft skills** such as teamwork and clear communication. | plural · neutral · opposite *hard / technical skills* · Grammar: impersonal passive |
| a career path | the series of jobs that make up sb's career | It is medicine, rather than the arts, that offers **a career path** with clearly defined stages. | C · neutral · *choose / follow a career path* · Grammar: cleft sentence |
| a career change | a move into a completely different type of work | Having made **a career change** at forty, he now works as a nurse in a busy emergency room. | C · neutral · *make a career change*; also *change careers* · Grammar: participle clause |
| a vocational certificate | a document showing that sb has trained for a practical job or trade | Aspiring plumbers can earn **a vocational certificate** in two years, thereby avoiding the cost of a college degree. | C · neutral · also *a trade certification*; BrE: *a vocational qualification*; NOT *a vocation certificate* · Grammar: result clause with thereby/thus |
| a degree | a qualification from a college or university | Not only does she have **a degree** in chemistry, but she also speaks three languages fluently. | C · neutral · *a degree in* + subject; NOT *a degree of chemistry* · Grammar: negative inversion |
| career counseling | professional guidance on choosing a job | Better **career counseling** should be offered in high schools so that students understand their options early. | U · neutral · person = *a career counselor*; BrE: *careers advice*, *a careers adviser* · Grammar: modal passive |
| an aptitude (for sth) | a natural ability to do sth well | The earlier teachers spot **an aptitude** for math, the more they can do to nurture it. | C/U · formal · *have an aptitude for* + noun / -ing · Grammar: the more…, the more… |
| a portfolio career | a career made up of several part-time jobs at once | While **a portfolio career** offers variety, it rarely provides the security of a single full-time job. | C · neutral · popularized in the UK, now used in AmE too · Grammar: concession |
| reskilling | learning new skills for a different job | Automation is replacing many routine jobs, which makes **reskilling** essential for older workers. | U · formal · compare *upskilling* = improving existing skills · Grammar: sentential relative |
| parental expectations | what parents hope or demand their children will achieve | If he hadn't tried to meet **parental expectations** by studying medicine, he might be a happy musician now. | plural · neutral · *meet / live up to parental expectations* · Grammar: mixed conditional |
| a reference | a person who can describe your work and character to a new employer | The best **references** are former supervisors whose firsthand knowledge of your work can reassure a new employer. | C · neutral · in AmE also the letter (*a letter of recommendation*); BrE: *a referee* (person) · Grammar: relative: whose / whereby / in which |

### Collocations
| Chunk | Meaning | Example | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| pursue a career in sth | try to build a career in a field | No matter how competitive the field is, she is determined to **pursue a career in** journalism. | formal · NOT *pursue a career of* · Grammar: no matter / however |
| follow your passion | do the work you love | I wish I could afford to **follow my passion**, but photography doesn't pay the rent. | neutral · Grammar: unreal past |
| learn a trade | train for a skilled practical job | Electricians are in such high demand that many teenagers now prefer to **learn a trade** instead of going to college. | neutral · *a trade* (C) = plumber, electrician, etc. · Grammar: so/such… that |
| a lucrative profession | a profession that pays very well | Should dentistry remain **a lucrative profession**, competition for admission to dental school will stay fierce. | C · formal · *lucrative* = profitable · Grammar: inversion conditional |
| bow to parental pressure | do what your parents want against your wishes | He should never have **bowed to parental pressure**; he hated law from his very first semester. | formal · also *give in to pressure* · Grammar: modal perfect |
| a strong work ethic | a belief in the value of hard work | Raised on a family farm, she developed **a strong work ethic** and never misses a deadline. | C · neutral · NOT *a strong working ethic* · Grammar: participle clause |
| a proven track record | clear evidence of past success | Candidates with **a proven track record** in sales are expected to receive the highest starting salaries. | C · neutral · *a track record of + -ing* · Grammar: impersonal passive |
| show initiative | act independently without being told | What impresses managers most is when junior staff **show initiative** without waiting to be told. | neutral · NOT *show an initiative*; *take the initiative* · Grammar: cleft sentence |
| be in high demand | be wanted by many employers | Software engineers **are in high demand**, which explains why their starting salaries keep rising. | neutral · opposite *be in low demand*; compare *be in short supply* (= too few available) · Grammar: sentential relative |
| retrain as sth | learn a new job | If he hadn't **retrained as** a teacher at thirty-five, he would still be stuck in a dead-end job. | neutral · *retrain as* + job; NOT *retrain to a teacher* · Grammar: mixed conditional |
| work well under pressure | stay effective when there is stress | Nursing is a profession in which you must **work well under pressure** every single day. | neutral · common in résumés and references · Grammar: relative: whose / whereby / in which |

| push sb into sth | force or strongly encourage sb to do sth | She wishes her parents hadn't **pushed her into** law, because she never enjoyed it. | neutral · *push sb into + -ing* · Grammar: unreal past |
| talk sb out of sth | persuade sb not to do sth | If my teacher hadn't **talked me out of** quitting school, I might never have gone to college. | neutral · opposite *talk sb into sth* · Grammar: modal perfect |
| carve out sth | create sth through effort over time | However crowded the market was, he managed to **carve out** a successful career as a translator. | neutral · *carve out a career / a niche / a role* · Grammar: no matter / however |

## Text 1 — Two-part question
> Many young people choose their future career mainly on the basis of salary rather than personal interest. Why do they do this, and is it a good idea?

Several pressures lead young people to put salary first. Higher education has become expensive: most English universities have charged £9,000 a year or more since 2012, so many graduates feel they must **go into a lucrative profession** simply to repay their loans. **Parental expectations** play a part too. Parents who struggled financially often **push** their children **into** medicine or law, and many teenagers **bow to parental pressure** rather than risk conflict at home. **In light of** rising rents, it is hardly surprising that money comes first.

**With regard to** whether this is wise, choosing a job for salary alone is, **as a rule**, a mistake. People who **pursue a career in** a field they find dull rarely excel, and many end up feeling like **a square peg in a round hole**. My cousin is **a case in point**: she trained as an accountant because it paid well, then **retrained as** an elementary school teacher at thirty after years of misery. Following one's passion blindly, though, is equally risky. A career counselor would be right to **talk** a teenager **out of** a field with almost no job openings **on the grounds that** enthusiasm alone will not pay the rent. The sensible approach is to **think through** both factors, choose work that **plays to** one's **strengths** and is **in high demand**, and develop **transferable skills** that allow **a career change** later.

**Notes**
- **Topic vocabulary:** *parental expectations* (plural), *transferable skills* (plural), *a career change* (C) — cause, safeguard and future option in one essay.
- **Collocations:** *go into a lucrative profession*, *pursue a career in* + field, *bow to parental pressure* — formal, precise alternatives to *choose a job*.
- **Collocations:** *be in high demand*, *retrain as* + job — useful for any essay on education, technology or the job market.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *push sb into sth* / *talk sb out of sth* — a natural pair for describing influence on decisions.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *think through* + a decision / the factors / the consequences — a good verb for your final recommendation (BrE also: *weigh up*).
- **Idioms:** *a square peg in a round hole*, *play to your strengths* — informal–neutral, acceptable once each in an essay.
- **Linkers:** *In light of + noun* (formal "considering"; BrE: *In the light of*), *As a rule* (= usually), *With regard to + noun / wh-clause* — *With regard to whether…* neatly opens the second half of a two-part answer.
- **Frames:** *X is a case in point: …* — introduces a short example; *on the grounds that + clause* — gives the reason for a decision or opinion.

## Text 2 — Letter (formal)
> A former colleague has applied for a job at another company and has asked you to write a reference. Write a letter to the company. In your letter: explain how you know this person; describe their skills and personal qualities; say why they would be suitable for the job.

Dear Sir or Madam,

I am writing to support Elena Petrova's application for the position of project coordinator. I was Elena's direct supervisor at Brightwell Accounting for four years, until she relocated in March.

**First and foremost**, Elena has **a strong work ethic**. She **hit the ground running** when she joined us and soon became the person colleagues turned to with difficult cases. She has **a proven track record** of delivering projects on time and **works well under pressure**, particularly at fiscal year-end.

Elena is also **a team player**, and while she was with us she regularly **showed initiative**; for example, she introduced a shared calendar that reduced missed deadlines. She is quick to give credit to others and, **by the same token**, accepts responsibility when things go wrong. **On the whole**, she needed very little supervision, although she was never too proud to ask for advice.

**It stands to reason that** someone with her **soft skills** and organizational ability would be an asset to a growing company like yours. **All things considered**, I have no hesitation in recommending her. For this role, she is **a safe bet**.

Sincerely,
James Morton
Director of Finance, Brightwell Accounting

**Notes**
- **Topic vocabulary:** *soft skills* (plural) — the qualities references focus on; pair with *organizational ability* (U).
- **Collocations:** *a strong work ethic*, *a proven track record (of + -ing)*, *work well under pressure*, *show initiative* (no article) — the four standard phrases of any reference or cover letter.
- **Idioms:** *hit the ground running*, *a team player*, *a safe bet* — positive, professional idioms that are acceptable in a formal reference.
- **Linkers:** *First and foremost, + most important point* — use it once, for your strongest point.
- **Linkers:** *By the same token, + parallel point* (formal); *On the whole, + clause* — a balanced general statement.
- **Linkers:** *It stands to reason that + clause* (= it is logical); *All things considered, + conclusion* — ideal just before a final recommendation.
- **Frames:** *I am writing to support X's application for the position of…*, *I have no hesitation in recommending her* — fixed formal phrases; *Dear Sir or Madam → Sincerely,* (BrE: *Yours faithfully*).
