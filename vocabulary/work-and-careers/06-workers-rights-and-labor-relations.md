# Workers' Rights & Labor Relations
*Work & Careers · labor laws and workers' rights, unions and strikes, exploitation and wage theft, workplace safety, harassment and discrimination, dismissal and whistleblowing, outsourcing and misclassified contractors*

## Vocabulary

### Key terms
| Chunk | Meaning | Example | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| workers' rights | the legal and moral rights people have at work, such as fair pay and safe conditions | Campaigners say **workers' rights** are under threat in the delivery industry. | plural · neutral · also *labor rights*; NOT *worker's rights* when you mean workers in general |
| labor laws | laws that control how employers must treat their staff | Strict **labor laws** limit how many hours teenagers can work. | plural · neutral · *labor law* (U) = the legal field; also *employment law*; BrE spelling *labour* |
| collective bargaining | negotiation between a union and an employer about pay and conditions | Through **collective bargaining**, the nurses won a 20% raise over four years. | U · formal · NOT *a collective bargaining*; *a collective bargaining agreement* (C) |
| worker exploitation | unfair treatment of workers so that sb else can profit from their work | The report exposed **worker exploitation** in the fashion supply chain. | U · neutral · also *the exploitation of workers*; adj *exploitative* (*exploitative conditions*); *corporate exploitation* is also used when blaming companies in general |
| fringe benefits | extras that come with a job on top of salary, such as health insurance or retirement contributions | The salary is modest, but the **fringe benefits**, including health insurance and a 401(k) match, make up for it. | plural · formal · informal: *perks*; see also *a benefits package* (04) |
| a labor dispute | a disagreement between workers and their employer about pay or conditions | **The labor dispute** at the port lasted three months. | C · formal · *settle / resolve a labor dispute*; BrE: *an industrial dispute* |
| a picket line | a line of striking workers outside a workplace who try to stop others from going in | Hundreds of nurses stood on **the picket line** in freezing rain. | C · neutral · *cross a picket line* (= keep working during a strike); verb *picket a factory* |
| a whistleblower | an employee who tells the authorities or the public about wrongdoing in their organization | **A whistleblower** revealed that the plant was dumping toxic waste. | C · neutral · *whistleblower protection* (U); verb *blow the whistle on sth* |
| wage theft | an employer's failure to pay workers money they have legally earned | Unpaid overtime is one of the most common forms of **wage theft**. | U · neutral · NOT *a wage theft*; includes unpaid overtime and stolen tips |
| wrongful termination | the illegal firing of an employee | She sued the company for **wrongful termination** after reporting fraud. | U · formal, legal · AmE; *be wrongfully terminated*; BrE: *unfair dismissal* |
| at-will employment | a US system in which an employer can fire a worker at any time for any reason that is not illegal, and the worker can quit at any time | Under **at-will employment**, most private-sector workers in the U.S. can be fired without warning. | U · formal · US; *an at-will employee* (C); not the rule in most other countries |
| union busting | an employer's efforts to stop workers from forming or joining a union | Amazon has been accused of **union busting** at its warehouses. | U · neutral · NOT *a union busting*; adj *union-busting tactics*; person *a union buster* (C) |
| a grievance procedure | an official process that lets employees complain about how they are treated | Staff should use **the grievance procedure** before going to court. | C · formal · *file / raise a grievance*; *a grievance* (C) = a formal complaint |
| sweatshop conditions | very bad working conditions with long hours, low pay and danger | Some garment workers still work in **sweatshop conditions**. | plural · neutral · *a sweatshop* (C) = a factory like this |
| a hostile work environment | a workplace where harassment or discrimination makes it hard for sb to do their job | Constant sexist jokes created **a hostile work environment**. | C · formal · US legal term; *create / allege a hostile work environment* |
| a labor shortage | a situation in which there are not enough workers available | The post-pandemic **labor shortage** pushed up wages in restaurants. | C · neutral · *face / ease a labor shortage*; opposite *a labor surplus* |

|---|---|---|---|

## Text 1 — Opinion (agree / disagree)
> Some people believe that labor unions are no longer necessary because governments now protect workers through labor laws. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

I strongly disagree that unions are no longer necessary. Good **labor laws** matter, but they set only minimum standards, and many of them **stem from** union campaigns in the first place.

The Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938, for example, **brought about** a federal minimum wage and overtime pay in the U.S., yet it says nothing about raises, health insurance or pensions. These are won through **collective bargaining**. In 2023, members of the United Auto Workers **walked out** at plants owned by all three Detroit automakers, and after about six weeks of strikes they won raises of 25% over four and a half years. No government would have ordered such an increase.

Unions also make existing laws work. A worker who wants to **file a complaint** about **wage theft** or **unpaid overtime** often stays silent, since under **at-will employment** most Americans can be fired for almost any reason. A union offers **strength in numbers** and **a grievance procedure**, so problems can be **ironed out** before they reach court.

I would not **rule out** the possibility that some unions protect poor performers, but that is a reason to reform them, not to abolish them. **In the long run**, **workers' rights** are safest when laws and unions support each other.

**Notes**
- **Topic vocabulary:** *labor laws* (plural), *workers' rights* (plural), *collective bargaining* (U, no article) — the three core terms for any question about unions.
- **Topic vocabulary:** *wage theft*, *unpaid overtime* (both U), *at-will employment* (U, US only) — concrete problems make the argument specific; *the three Detroit automakers* (often called *the Big Three*) = Ford, General Motors and Stellantis.
- **Collocations:** *file a complaint (about sth / with an agency)*, *a grievance procedure* (C) — the formal routes for solving problems at work.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *walk out* (intransitive; noun *a walkout*) — a strong alternative to *go on strike*: *walk out at a plant / over pay*.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *stem from* + noun (= be caused by; never passive), *bring about* + change (= cause to happen; formal) — two key verbs for cause and effect.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *iron out* + problems / differences (= solve small problems); *rule out* + a possibility (= decide sth is impossible).
- **Idioms:** *strength in numbers* — the central idea of unions in three words.
- **Linkers:** *In the long run, + clause* — introduces a long-term conclusion.
- **Frames:** *I would not rule out the possibility that…, but that is a reason to reform X, not to abolish it.* — a polite concession followed by a firm reply to the opposing view.

## Text 2 — Letter (formal)
> Your manager regularly asks staff to work extra hours without pay. Write a letter to your company's human resources manager. In your letter: describe the situation; explain how it is affecting you and your colleagues; say what you would like the company to do.

Dear Ms. Lopez,

I am writing to bring to your attention a problem with **unpaid overtime** in the customer service department, where I have worked for three years.

Since March, our team leader has regularly asked us to stay an extra hour or two after our shifts. These hours are not recorded, so we are effectively **working off the clock**. As hourly employees, we are legally entitled to be paid for all the hours we work, so I am concerned that this practice may amount to **wage theft**. The problem seems to have **come about** because the team has been short-staffed since two colleagues left.

The situation is affecting all of us. Several colleagues are exhausted, and two are looking for new jobs. Others are unwilling to **speak out** **for fear of** losing their jobs.

I would be grateful if you could **look into** this matter and make sure that all our hours are recorded and paid. I would also welcome a meeting to **work out** a fairer schedule. I would much prefer to resolve this informally rather than use the formal **grievance procedure**, and I am confident that we can **reach an agreement** that works for everyone.

Sincerely,
Andrew Kim

**Notes**
- **Topic vocabulary:** *unpaid overtime*, *wage theft* (both U, no article) — *this practice may amount to wage theft* is a careful, formal way to suggest that something is illegal; *work off the clock* (AmE) names the practice precisely.
- **Collocations:** *the (formal) grievance procedure* (C), *reach an agreement* (NOT *achieve an agreement*) — a reasonable tone makes a complaint letter more persuasive.
- **Collocations:** *be legally entitled to + noun / to be + past participle* — *entitled to overtime pay*, *entitled to be paid*.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *speak out (against sth)* (intransitive) — *unwilling / afraid to speak out*.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *come about* (= happen, usually of a problem or change; no passive): *How did this come about?*
- **Phrasal verbs:** *look into* + a matter / complaint (= investigate), *work out* + a schedule / solution (= find by discussion) — two standard verbs for the request paragraph.
- **Frames:** *I am writing to bring to your attention…*, *I would be grateful if you could…*, *I would much prefer to X rather than Y* — formal openers and requests; *Dear Ms. + last name → Sincerely,* (BrE: *Yours sincerely*).
- **Frames:** *for fear of + -ing* (formal: = because they are afraid of) — *for fear of losing their jobs / being fired*.
