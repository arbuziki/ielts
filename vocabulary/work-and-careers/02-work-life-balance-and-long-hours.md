# Work-Life Balance & Long Hours
*Work & Careers · overtime, burnout, a long-hours culture, the right to disconnect, the four-day workweek, family time, productivity vs hours worked, vacation and leave*

## Vocabulary

### Key terms
| Chunk | Meaning | Example | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| burnout | physical and mental exhaustion caused by too much work or stress | Teachers are leaving the profession because of **burnout**. | U · neutral · *suffer from burnout*; NOT *a burnout* in essays |
| a long-hours culture | a workplace attitude that expects people to work very long hours | Many law firms still have **a long-hours culture**. | C · neutral · also *a culture of overwork* |
| overtime | extra hours worked beyond normal hours | Nurses often work unpaid **overtime**. | U · neutral · *work / do overtime*; NOT *overtimes* |
| presenteeism | staying at work longer than needed, or working while sick, to be seen | **Presenteeism** costs companies more than sick leave. | U · formal · opposite *absenteeism* |
| the right to disconnect | a legal right not to answer work emails outside working hours | France introduced **the right to disconnect** in 2017. | C, usually *the* · formal |
| a four-day workweek | a schedule of four working days a week, usually with no loss of pay | Several firms have moved to **a four-day workweek**. | C · neutral · BrE: *a four-day (working) week* |
| paid time off | time away from work for which you are still paid | Staff must use their **paid time off** before December. | U · neutral · abbreviation *PTO*; often covers vacation and sick days; BrE: *annual leave* |
| vacation days | the paid days off a worker gets each year | Many U.S. employers offer only about ten **vacation days** a year. | plural · neutral · *use / take vacation days*; the U.S. has no legal minimum, while UK workers are entitled to 28 days; BrE: *holiday entitlement* |
| productivity | the amount of work done in relation to time or cost | Tired staff cause **productivity** to fall. | U · neutral · *boost / raise / improve productivity* |
| a workaholic | a person who works too much and finds it hard to stop | My boss is **a workaholic** who answers emails at midnight. | C · informal–neutral |
| work-related stress | stress caused by a person's job | **Work-related stress** is a leading cause of sick leave. | U · neutral · also *occupational stress* (formal) |
| karoshi | (Japanese) death caused by overwork | In Japan, families can claim compensation for **karoshi**. | U · formal · loanword; explain it when you use it |

## Text 1 — Discuss both views
> Some people think that working long hours is necessary to succeed in a career, while others believe it damages people's health and family life. Discuss both views and give your opinion.

Supporters of **a long-hours culture** argue that career success **hinges on** commitment, and that staying late is the clearest way to show it. In competitive fields such as law and banking, junior staff routinely **put in long hours**, and some firms use this pressure to **weed out** less determined recruits. For young people on modest starting salaries, **overtime** may also be the only way to earn enough to **live on**.

However, I side with those who see long hours as harmful. Beyond a certain point, extra work brings **diminishing returns**: tired employees make more mistakes, and many eventually **burn out**. In Japan, death from overwork is common enough to have its own name, **karoshi**. Family life suffers too, as parents who **stay behind** every evening see little of their children. Worse still, **presenteeism** rewards being seen rather than achieving results.

There is growing evidence that **shorter working hours** need not hurt a business. In a 2022 British trial of **a four-day workweek**, 56 of the 61 participating companies chose to continue with it once the six-month trial ended. In my view, success **boils down to** what people achieve, not how long they sit at a desk. Instead of constantly **moving up** deadlines, managers should plan realistic schedules so that staff can **switch off** in the evenings and return refreshed.

**Notes**
- **Topic vocabulary:** *a long-hours culture*, *overtime* (U), *presenteeism* (U) — name the problem precisely instead of *working a lot*.
- **Topic vocabulary:** *karoshi*, *a four-day workweek* — concrete examples that make an argument specific; always explain a foreign word.
- **Collocations:** *put in long hours*, *shorter working hours*, *diminishing returns* — the last one works for any "more is not always better" argument (screen time, homework, spending).
- **Phrasal verbs:** *burn out*, *stay behind*, *switch off* — everyday but neutral, fine in Task 2.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *hinge on* + noun (formal), *boil down to* + noun / wh-clause — two ways to say "depend on / be basically about".
- **Phrasal verbs:** *weed out* + weak candidates / errors; *move up* + a deadline / date (BrE: *bring forward*; opposite *push back*); *live on* + a salary / pension.
- **Frame:** *Supporters of X argue that…* / *However, I side with those who…* — a clean way to move from the first view to your own.
- **Frame:** *Beyond a certain point, …* — introduces a limit; reusable for technology, tourism, consumption.
- **Frame:** *X need not + inf* — *shorter hours need not hurt a business*; a formal way to reject a fear.

## Text 2 — Letter (informal)
> A friend of yours has been working very long hours for months and has not taken any vacation. Write a letter to your friend. In your letter: say why you are worried about them; remind them why a break matters; suggest going away together for a few days.

Hi Tom,

It was great to see you at Mike's birthday, but I have to admit I was a bit worried afterward. You looked exhausted, and when you said you hadn't **taken time off** since January, I could hardly believe it.

I know you're **snowed under** right now, and I remember what it's like – I was **up to my ears in** exams last spring and nearly **ran myself into the ground**. But you can't keep **burning the candle at both ends** forever. You've stopped coming to soccer, you never answer texts, and you seem completely **cut off** from everyone. Honestly, if the project slips by a few days, **it's not the end of the world**.

You must have a lot of **paid time off** left, so why not use some of it? My parents' cabin by the lake is free the first week of June. We could go hiking, cook big dinners and properly **recharge our batteries** – no laptops allowed! A real break would help you think clearly again, and you'd probably get more done afterward. You've earned it.

Let me know what you think, and **hang in there** till then!

Take care,
Alex

**Notes**
- **Collocations:** *take time off* — the natural way to talk about a vacation from work; NOT *make a vacation*.
- **Topic vocabulary:** *paid time off* (U, often *PTO*) — no *a*, no plural; *I have ten days of PTO left* (BrE: *annual leave*).
- **Idioms:** *burn the candle at both ends*, *run yourself into the ground*, *recharge your batteries* — perfect for informal letters, too casual for essays.
- **Fixed expressions:** *be snowed under (with work)*, *be up to my / your ears in + work / exams* — two informal ways to say "very busy" (BrE also: *up to my eyes in*).
- **Fixed expressions:** *It's not the end of the world (if…)* — reassures; *Hang in there!* — encourages and makes a natural informal closer.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *be / feel cut off from* + people / society — useful for loneliness, rural life, remote work.
- **Frames:** *I have to admit…*, *so why not + verb?*, *Let me know what you think* — chatty openers, suggestions and endings for any informal letter; *Hi + first name → Take care, / Best,*.
