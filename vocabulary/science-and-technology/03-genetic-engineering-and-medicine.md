# Genetic Engineering & Medicine
*Science & Technology · gene editing, preventing inherited diseases, designer babies, GM crops, DNA testing, medical advances and longer life expectancy, ethical and safety concerns*

## Vocabulary

### Key terms
| Chunk | Meaning | Example | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| genetic engineering | changing the genes of a living thing | **Genetic engineering** is expected to make many staple crops more resistant to pests and disease. | U, no article; neutral · Grammar: impersonal passive |
| gene editing | making precise changes to DNA, e.g. with the CRISPR tool | Already approved for sickle cell disease, **gene editing** could soon treat other inherited blood disorders. | U; tool: *CRISPR* · Grammar: participle clause |
| gene therapy | treating a disease by changing or replacing faulty genes | Although **gene therapy** is extremely expensive, it offers real hope to children with rare blood disorders. | U · Grammar: concession |
| an inherited disease | an illness passed from parents to children through genes | Cystic fibrosis is **an inherited disease** in which thick mucus builds up in the lungs. | C; formal synonym: *a hereditary condition* · Grammar: relative: whose / whereby / in which |
| a faulty gene | a gene with a mistake in it that can cause illness | If she hadn't been born with **a faulty gene**, she wouldn't need daily treatment for her lungs now. | C; also *a defective gene* · Grammar: mixed conditional |
| a designer baby | a baby whose genes have been chosen by its parents | Not only could gene editing lead to **designer babies**, but it could also deepen inequality between rich and poor. | C; usually critical · Grammar: negative inversion |
| an embryo | a human or animal at the earliest stage of development | **An embryo** can be tested for serious genetic diseases before it is implanted during IVF. | C; adjective: *embryonic* · Grammar: modal passive |
| GM crops | crops whose genes have been changed | Public suspicion of **GM crops** in Europe is so strong that few supermarkets will stock genetically modified food. | C, plural; full form: *genetically modified crops*; AmE also: *GMO crops*; *GM food* (U) · Grammar: so/such… that |
| DNA | the substance in cells that carries genetic information | My sister wishes she had never sent her **DNA** to a testing company, since its database was later hacked. | U, no article; but *a DNA test* (C) · Grammar: unreal past |
| life expectancy | the average number of years a person is expected to live | Vaccines and antibiotics have saved millions of children, thereby raising **life expectancy** around the world. | U; *rise / fall*; NOT *life expectation* · Grammar: result clause with thereby/thus |
| a side effect | an unwanted extra effect of a drug or treatment | What worries patients most about the new therapy is the risk of serious **side effects**. | C, often plural · Grammar: cleft sentence |
| a genetic predisposition to sth | a higher natural chance of developing a condition | He has **a genetic predisposition to** heart disease, which is why his doctor checks his blood pressure every year. | C, singular; formal; + *to* + noun · Grammar: sentential relative |

## Text 1 — Advantages and disadvantages
> Genetic engineering may allow scientists to prevent many serious diseases, but some people are worried about the risks. Do the advantages of genetic engineering outweigh the disadvantages?

Few medical advances **raise** as many **ethical concerns** as **genetic engineering**, yet I believe its benefits clearly outweigh its risks, provided that it is tightly regulated.

The strongest argument in its favor is that it can tackle the cause of **inherited diseases** rather than their symptoms. In 2023 the UK became the first country to approve a **gene-editing** treatment, Casgevy, for sickle cell disease, offering patients who had **given up** hope of a normal life the chance to live without constant pain. Such patients **make up** a small share of the population, but their care **uses up** a great deal of hospital time. In farming, **drought-resistant crops** could **boost crop yields** in regions hit by climate change. Banning these tools would mean **throwing away** **a medical breakthrough** of historic importance.

The dangers, admittedly, are real. Changing **embryos** could have **unforeseen consequences** for future generations, and many fear **a slippery slope** toward **designer babies**. The case of He Jiankui, the Chinese scientist jailed in 2019 for editing the genes of twin girls, shows that some researchers are willing to **play God**.

These problems, however, call for **strict safeguards** rather than a ban. Governments should not **give in to** pressure from either frightened voters or biotech firms; instead, they should **err on the side of caution**, allowing therapies for serious illness while prohibiting changes that could be passed to children.

**Notes**
- **Topic vocabulary:** *genetic engineering*, *gene editing*, *inherited diseases*, *embryos*, *designer babies* — the five nouns every gene question needs; the first two are U, no article, but hyphenated before a noun: *a gene-editing treatment*.
- **Collocations:** *raise ethical concerns*, *unforeseen consequences*, *strict safeguards* — the "risks" side in neutral, formal language.
- **Collocations:** *drought-resistant crops*, *boost crop yields* — lets you widen the essay from medicine to food.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *give up hope* (*give up* + noun / -ing); *give in to* + pressure / demands.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *make up* + a small share / a large proportion of + noun; *use up* + hospital time / resources / savings.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *throw away* + an opportunity / a breakthrough — *Banning X would mean throwing away…* is a strong anti-ban frame.
- **Fixed expressions & frames:** *play God*, *a slippery slope* — give the opposing view in idiomatic form; one each is enough.
- **Fixed expressions & frames:** *err on the side of caution* — a balanced, formal recommendation for any risky technology.
- **Fixed expressions & frames:** *These problems, however, call for X rather than a ban.* — reusable for AI, social media, alcohol, cars.
- **Fixed expressions & frames:** *provided that it is tightly regulated* — a condition that makes your position sound mature; *in its favor* (BrE: *favour*).

## Text 2 — Letter (informal)
> Your friend is thinking of doing a home DNA test to find out about their family history and health risks. You did one last year. Write a letter to your friend. In your letter
> - describe your experience
> - mention anything you were surprised or worried about
> - give your advice.

Hi Sam,

Great to hear from you! You asked about my DNA test, so here's the full story.

It couldn't have been easier: I spat into a tube, mailed it off and got my results online about six weeks later. The family history part was fascinating — apparently I'm a quarter Scandinavian, which nobody in the family can explain!

The health report was a different story. It said I had **a genetic predisposition to** type 2 diabetes. The email arrived just after I'd **come down with** a nasty cold, so I was already **feeling run down**, and I completely panicked. It took me a few days to **get over** the shock. In the end my family doctor **ruled out** anything serious and explained that a higher risk isn't a diagnosis. Besides, diabetes **runs in** my dad's **family**, so it was hardly news.

My advice? Go for it, but read the privacy policy before you send off a sample of your **DNA**, and don't open the health results when you're **feeling under the weather**. And remember, knowing your risks won't **make up for** a lack of exercise — I've started biking to work!

Let me know what you decide.

Take care,
Anna

**Notes**
- **Topic vocabulary:** *DNA* (U, no article) vs *a DNA test* (C) — a classic article trap.
- **Topic vocabulary:** *a genetic predisposition to* + illness — sounds precise even in an informal letter; note *to*, not *for*.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *come down with* + a cold / the flu; *get over* + the shock / an illness (informal).
- **Phrasal verbs:** *rule out* + anything serious / a diagnosis — useful in any health letter; *my family doctor* (BrE: *my GP*).
- **Phrasal verbs:** *make up for* + a lack of + noun — *won't make up for a lack of exercise / sleep / experience*.
- **Fixed expressions & frames:** *feel run down*, *feel under the weather* — two informal ways to say "slightly sick"; do not use them in essays.
- **Fixed expressions & frames:** *run in the family* — split naturally as *runs in my dad's family*.
- **Fixed expressions & frames:** *It couldn't have been easier* / *The… part was a different story* — lively informal structure for describing any experience; *mailed it off* (BrE: *posted it off*).
- **Fixed expressions & frames:** *My advice? Go for it, but…* — a natural informal way into the advice bullet; open with *Hi Sam,* and close with *Take care,* / *Talk soon,* / *Best,*.
