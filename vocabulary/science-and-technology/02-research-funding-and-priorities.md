# Research Funding & Priorities
*Science & Technology · government vs private funding of science, profit-driven research, basic vs applied research, medical research priorities, the brain drain, ethics and independence of scientists*

## Vocabulary

### Key terms
| Chunk | Meaning | Example | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| basic research | research done to understand how things work, with no immediate practical use | **Basic research** into bacteria eventually led to gene editing. | U, no article; also *fundamental research* |
| applied research | research aimed at solving a particular practical problem | Most company money goes into **applied research**. | U, no article |
| blue-sky research | research with no obvious short-term application | Few investors are willing to pay for **blue-sky research**. | U; neutral; also *curiosity-driven research* |
| a research grant | money given to a scientist or team for a project | She won **a research grant** to study malaria. | C; *apply for / award / receive a grant* |
| public funding | money provided by the government | **Public funding** for science has fallen in real terms. | U: NOT *public fundings* |

| a conflict of interest | a situation in which sb's private interests could affect their judgment | Funding from a tobacco company creates **a conflict of interest**. | C; *disclose a conflict of interest*; BrE spelling: *judgement* |

## Text 1 — Discuss both views and give your opinion
> Some people believe that scientific research should be funded mainly by governments, while others think it should be paid for by private companies. Discuss both views and give your opinion.

Opinions differ on who should pay for science, but I believe governments should remain its main funders.

Supporters of private funding argue that companies are better at **turning** discoveries **into** products. Driven by **the profit motive**, they **pour** vast sums **into** **applied research** and move quickly from the laboratory to the market; the speed with which drug companies produced COVID-19 vaccines in 2020 **stands out** as an example.

However, this argument **glosses over** an obvious problem: companies only pay for work that is **commercially viable**. They **steer clear of** **neglected diseases**, whose patients cannot afford expensive drugs, and of **basic research**, whose payoff is uncertain. There is also the risk of **a conflict of interest**, since **he who pays the piper calls the tune**, and some firms have **suppressed unfavorable results**. Governments, by contrast, can fund work **in the public interest** and protect **academic freedom**, so that scientists can **stand up for** inconvenient findings. The World Wide Web, invented in 1989 at CERN, a publicly funded laboratory, and given away free, shows what such research can achieve.

For these reasons, public money should remain the backbone of science, with companies contributing through partnerships. If **public funding** **dries up**, many gifted young researchers will **drop out of** science altogether, and those who stay will be **lured away** by whoever pays most.

**Notes**
- **Topic vocabulary:** *applied research* vs *basic research* — the key contrast for any funding question; both U, no article.
- **Topic vocabulary:** *neglected diseases*, *a conflict of interest* — the two strongest arguments against relying on business.
- **Collocations:** *the profit motive*, *commercially viable*, *suppress unfavorable results* — formal, precise ways to criticize companies without sounding emotional (BrE: *unfavourable*, *criticise*).
- **Collocations:** *in the public interest*, *academic freedom* — the government side in two chunks.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *stand out as an example* — a neat way to introduce evidence; *gloss over* + a problem / a weakness (= treat it as unimportant).
- **Phrasal verbs:** *steer clear of* + an area / a risk (= avoid); *stand up for* + findings / rights; *drop out of* + science / school / college.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *pour sth into*, *dry up*, *lure sb away* — vivid but neutral; fine in essays.
- **Fixed expressions & frames:** *he who pays the piper calls the tune* — a well-known proverb (more common in BrE; AmE speakers also say *money talks*); use it once, for sponsorship, advertising or media ownership too.
- **Fixed expressions & frames:** *X, invented in [year] at [place], shows what such Y can achieve.* — a compact frame for a real example.
- **Fixed expressions & frames:** *Supporters of X argue that… However, this argument glosses over an obvious problem:…* — a reusable shape for "discuss both views".

## Text 2 — Essay paragraph — the case for basic research
> Develop a different angle: governments should fund basic, curiosity-driven research with no immediate commercial use, because many major breakthroughs began this way.

There is a strong case for funding **basic research** even when no practical use is in sight. Penicillin is often **singled out** as the classic example: Alexander Fleming **stumbled upon** it in 1928 while studying bacteria, not while searching for a medicine, yet antibiotics later turned infections that once **wiped out** whole families into minor illnesses. A more recent case **backs up** the same point. For years, microbiologists studied how bacteria defend themselves against viruses, work that no company would have paid for. In 2012, Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier showed that this defense system, CRISPR, could be used to edit genes, and the discovery earned them the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2020. **Blue-sky research** of this kind also **breaks down** the barriers between disciplines, since nobody knows in advance which field will provide the answer. The danger is that governments, under pressure to show results, demand **a return on investment** within a few years. If they do, promising lines of inquiry will **peter out** long before they **bear fruit**. Science has always advanced by **trial and error**, and **research grants** awarded **with no strings attached** are the price of the next unexpected breakthrough.

**Notes**
- **Topic vocabulary:** *basic research*, *blue-sky research*, *research grants* — use *blue-sky* once to vary the key term.
- **Collocations:** *a return on investment* — note *on*; frames the government's impatience in economic language.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *single out* + sth *as* + noun (formal) — *X is often singled out as the classic example* introduces any famous case.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *back up* + a point / a claim (+ *with evidence*) — *A more recent case backs up the same point* links two examples smoothly.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *wipe out* + families / species / savings; *peter out* (intransitive) = gradually stop: lines of inquiry / a campaign / a conversation (BrE also: *lines of enquiry*).
- **Phrasal verbs:** *break down* + barriers between X; *stumble upon* + a discovery.
- **Fixed expressions & frames:** *bear fruit*, *trial and error*, *with no strings attached* — three set phrases that sound natural, not ornate.
- **Fixed expressions & frames:** *There is a strong case for + -ing… even when…* — a confident topic sentence for any "should the government fund X" question.
- **Fixed expressions & frames:** *…are the price of the next unexpected breakthrough* — a memorable but plain closing line.
