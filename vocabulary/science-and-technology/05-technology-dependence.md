## Text 1 — Two-part question
> People are becoming increasingly dependent on technology in their daily lives. Why is this happening, and is it a positive or negative development?

The main reason for our growing **reliance on technology** is simple **convenience**. A smartphone has replaced the map, the address book and the bank branch, offering **instant access to information** **at the touch of a button**. Businesses encourage this shift because apps help them **keep** costs **down**: a bank that moves its customers online can **get rid of** expensive branches. Social pressure matters too: teenagers who are not on their friends' apps feel they do not **fit in** and worry about **missing out on** invitations.

On balance, I see this as a positive development, but one with a serious weakness. Technology saves time and brings services to people who live far from a town. The danger is that we are **putting all our eggs in one basket**. In July 2024, a faulty software update from the security company CrowdStrike crashed millions of Windows computers, grounding flights and disrupting hospitals worldwide, and many organizations had no paper systems to **fall back on**. There is a quieter cost too: when every trip is planned by an app, people **lose the ability to** read a map.

The answer is not to reject technology but to keep basic skills alive. Families might **cut down on** **screen time** on the weekend, and schools should continue to teach **mental math** and map reading, so that **a system failure** becomes an inconvenience rather than a disaster.

**Notes**
- **Topic vocabulary:** *reliance on technology* (note *on*), *convenience*, *screen time* — all U, no article.
- **Collocations:** *instant access to information*, *lose the ability to* + inf — the main benefit and the main cost in two chunks.
- **Collocations:** *a system failure*, *mental math* — concrete nouns that make the risk real (BrE: *mental arithmetic*).
- **Phrasal verbs:** *keep* + costs / prices + *down*; *get rid of* + branches / staff / waste.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *fit in* (intransitive) and *miss out on* + invitations / opportunities — the social reasons, useful for any youth topic.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *cut down on* + screen time / sugar / car use; *fall back on* + paper systems / savings / skills.
- **Fixed expressions & frames:** *at the touch of a button* — the standard phrase for ease of use; *put all our eggs in one basket* — the standard phrase for dependence on one system.
- **Fixed expressions & frames:** *The main reason for X is simple Y.* — a direct opening for the "why" part of any two-part question.
- **Fixed expressions & frames:** *On balance, I see this as a positive development, but one with a serious weakness.* — a nuanced verdict you can reuse almost word for word.
- **Fixed expressions & frames:** *…so that X becomes an inconvenience rather than a disaster* — a closing line for any risk-management solution; *on the weekend* (BrE: *at weekends*).

## Text 2 — Essay paragraph — technology frees us for more valuable skills
> Develop the opposing argument: relying on technology is no different from past reliance on books or calculators, and it frees people to focus on more valuable skills.

Fears about our **reliance on technology** are nothing new. In Plato's *Phaedrus*, Socrates warns that writing will weaken people's memory, because they will trust marks on a page instead of their own minds, which is exactly the complaint now made about search engines. A generation ago, teachers feared that calculators would destroy **mental math**; today calculators are allowed in many math exams, and few would argue that students **lose out** as a result. Each time, people **outsourced** a routine task and gained time for something more demanding. An engineer no longer calculates loads by hand, for example; her software checks that a design **complies with** safety regulations, leaving her free to concentrate on solving problems creatively. Admittedly, we should all **abide by** one sensible rule, **use it or lose it**, and practice the few skills we would need in an emergency. But to **adhere to** old methods simply because they are familiar makes little sense. Schools that **stick to** hours of long division would do better to teach **digital literacy**: how to judge sources, protect personal data and know when to **switch off**. Technology, like the printed book before it, is a tool, and what matters is how we use the time it saves.

**Notes**
- **Topic vocabulary:** *digital literacy* — the positive alternative to "banning phones"; U, no article.
- **Collocations:** *outsource* + a task / your memory — a precise verb for handing work to a machine.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *lose out* (intransitive, + *on sth* / *to sb*) — *few would argue that X lose out as a result*.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *comply with* + regulations / standards (formal); *abide by* + a rule / the law / a decision (formal).
- **Phrasal verbs:** *adhere to* + methods / guidelines / principles (formal); *stick to* + a plan / a method (neutral) — four ways to say "follow", so you never repeat one.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *switch off* (= stop paying attention) — closes the list with an everyday verb.
- **Fixed expressions & frames:** *use it or lose it* — informal-neutral; works for languages, fitness, memory. In AmE, *practice* is both noun and verb (BrE verb: *practise*).
- **Fixed expressions & frames:** *Fears about X are nothing new.* — a historical opening for any "is this new technology harmful?" question.
- **Fixed expressions & frames:** *Admittedly, … But to … simply because … makes little sense.* — a compact concession-and-rebuttal pattern.
- **Fixed expressions & frames:** *X, like Y before it, is a tool, and what matters is how we use it.* — a reusable concluding line for technology essays; *math exams* (BrE: *maths exams*).
