# Space Exploration
*Science & Technology · the cost of space programs, private space companies, satellites and spin-offs, colonizing other planets, space tourism, priorities on Earth vs in space*

## Vocabulary

### Key terms
| Chunk | Meaning | Example | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| space exploration | the investigation of space using spacecraft, probes and telescopes | It is often argued that **space exploration** wastes public money, yet it has given us weather satellites and countless medical spin-offs. | U, no article; neutral · Grammar: impersonal passive |
| a space program | a country's long-term plan of space missions | If India hadn't invested in **a space program** decades ago, it wouldn't now be able to launch satellites so cheaply. | C; BrE: *a space programme* · Grammar: mixed conditional |
| a space agency | a government body that runs space missions, e.g. NASA or ESA | NASA is **a space agency** whose budget makes up less than half a percent of US federal spending. | C; capital letters in names: *the European Space Agency* · Grammar: relative: whose / whereby / in which |
| a satellite | an object sent into orbit to collect or send information | Orbiting hundreds of miles above the Earth, **a satellite** can photograph an entire region in minutes and track approaching storms. | C; also used as an adjective: *satellite images* · Grammar: participle clause |
| a space probe | a spacecraft without a crew, sent to study distant planets | Not until 2012 did **a space probe** finally leave the solar system, when Voyager 1 crossed into interstellar space. | C; neutral · Grammar: negative inversion |
| a crewed mission | a space flight with astronauts on board | Even though **a crewed mission** costs far more than a robotic one, astronauts can make quick decisions that machines cannot. | C; neutral; older form: *a manned mission* · Grammar: concession |
| a spin-off | a useful product that develops from research done for a different purpose | It was NASA research that gave us memory foam, perhaps the most famous **spin-off** from the space program. | C; hyphenated noun; *a spin-off from sth* · Grammar: cleft sentence |
| space debris | broken satellites and rocket parts left in orbit | The more satellites companies launch, the more **space debris** builds up in orbit around the Earth. | U: NOT *debrises*; informal: *space junk* · Grammar: the more…, the more… |
| space tourism | paying to travel into space for pleasure | **Space tourism** remains so expensive that only the super-rich can afford a few minutes of weightlessness. | U, no article; neutral · Grammar: so/such… that |
| a reusable rocket | a rocket that can land and fly again | SpaceX developed **a reusable rocket** that lands upright after each flight, thereby cutting launch costs dramatically. | C; opposite: *an expendable rocket* · Grammar: result clause with thereby/thus |
| orbit | the curved path of an object around a planet | Old satellites should be removed from **orbit** before they break apart and create dangerous debris. | U in *in orbit / into orbit* (no article); C in *complete an orbit* · Grammar: modal passive |
| the space race | the competition between the US and the USSR to reach space first | The Soviet Union launched Sputnik in 1957, which marked the start of **the space race** with the United States. | with *the*; also *a new space race* (today's rivals) · Grammar: sentential relative |

## Text 1 — Opinion (to what extent do you agree or disagree)
> Some people think that spending money on space exploration is a waste when there are so many problems on Earth. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

It is easy to see why **space exploration** strikes some people as an extravagance while millions still lack clean water or basic health care. Nevertheless, I largely disagree that it is **money down the drain**.

Those who call **a space program** **a drain on public resources** rarely **look into** the actual figures. NASA receives less than half a percent of the US federal budget, and India's Chandrayaan-3 mission, which **touched down** near the Moon's south pole in 2023, reportedly cost less than the Hollywood movie *Interstellar*. Scrapping such projects would not **free up** enough money to end poverty, because the **pressing problems** on Earth stem mainly from poor governance rather than a shortage of cash. A government can **look after** its citizens and **push the boundaries of knowledge** at the same time; the task is to **work out** a sensible balance, not to choose one or the other.

Space research also shapes how young people see science. Many of today's engineers grew up **looking up to** astronauts, and a successful **crewed mission** can **inspire a generation** in a way no textbook can. Nor should we **look down on** **space agencies** in poorer countries as vanity projects: their **satellites** monitor crops, floods and cyclones for millions of farmers.

In short, governments must **get their priorities right**, but **the final frontier** deserves a modest place among them.

**Notes**
- **Topic vocabulary:** *a space program*, *space agencies*, *a crewed mission*, *satellites* — precise nouns instead of vague *space things*; *crewed* is the modern neutral word. BrE: *programme*.
- **Collocations:** *a drain on public resources*, *pressing problems* — the critics' case in two chunks; *a drain on* also works for prisons, subsidies, an aging population.
- **Collocations:** *push the boundaries of knowledge*, *inspire a generation* — two ready-made benefits that also fit questions about the arts, sports and higher education.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *look into* + the figures / a complaint = investigate; *work out* + a balance / how to + inf.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *look after* + citizens / your health (AmE also *take care of*); *look up to* + role models; *look down on* + sth *as* + noun (critical attitude).
- **Phrasal verbs:** *touch down* (intransitive); *free up* + money / time / space.
- **Fixed expressions & frames:** *money down the drain* is semi-informal, so use it once and ideally when giving the opposing view; *get their priorities right* — note *their* changes with the subject.
- **Fixed expressions & frames:** *Those who call X Y rarely look into the actual figures.* — a reusable opener for any rebuttal.
- **Fixed expressions & frames:** *the task is to work out a sensible balance, not to choose one or the other* — fits any "X or Y?" question.
- **Fixed expressions & frames:** *reportedly cost less than…* — *reportedly* hedges a figure you read in the news; AmE *percent* is one word (BrE also *per cent*).

## Text 2 — Essay paragraph — practical benefits justify the cost
> Develop the opposing argument: space research has delivered practical everyday benefits (satellite navigation, weather forecasting, medical technology) that justify its cost.

When we **weigh** the cost of space research against what it has given us, the balance is clearly positive. Every time we use **satellite navigation** or check tomorrow's weather, we rely on **satellites** that governments have **put into orbit**, and modern **weather forecasting** gives coastal towns days of warning before a hurricane arrives. Medicine has gained too. **Spin-offs** from **space programs** include memory foam, first developed in the 1960s for NASA aircraft seats, and the infrared ear thermometers used in hospitals, which borrow technology designed to measure the temperature of stars. None of these benefits was planned, which is precisely why space research cannot be judged by short-term results: the money a nation **sets aside** for it tends to **pay off** only **in the long run**. The industry is also becoming more efficient: private firms have **brought down** **launch costs** with **reusable rockets**, and the savings are increasingly **passed on** to universities and small countries that want to **launch** their own **satellites**. Finally, the expertise built up in each mission is **handed down** to the next generation of engineers, keeping a country's high-tech industry alive. A budget that achieves all this can hardly be called wasteful.

**Notes**
- **Topic vocabulary:** *spin-offs*, *launch costs*, *reusable rockets* — the three nouns that turn a vague "space is useful" into a concrete argument.
- **Collocations:** *put satellites into orbit*, *launch a satellite* — no article before *orbit*.
- **Collocations:** *satellite navigation*, *weather forecasting* — both U; say *a weather forecast* only for one forecast.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *weigh* + the cost + *against* + the benefits — a clean opening for any cost-benefit paragraph: *When we weigh X against Y, …* (BrE often *weigh up*).
- **Phrasal verbs:** *set aside* + money / time / land; *pay off* (intransitive: *the investment paid off*).
- **Phrasal verbs:** *pass on* + savings / costs + *to* + sb; *hand down* + expertise / traditions + *to the next generation*.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *bring down* + costs / prices — neutral alternative to *reduce*.
- **Fixed expressions & frames:** *in the long run* — use once per essay; the natural partner of *pay off*.
- **Fixed expressions & frames:** *None of these benefits was planned, which is precisely why X cannot be judged by short-term results* — a strong frame for defending research, education or culture spending.
- **Fixed expressions & frames:** *A budget that achieves all this can hardly be called wasteful.* — a concluding sentence you can adapt: *A policy that… can hardly be called…*
