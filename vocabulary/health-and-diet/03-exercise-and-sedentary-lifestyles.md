# Exercise & Sedentary Lifestyles
*Health & Diet · lack of physical activity, desk jobs, car dependence, sports in schools, gyms and public sports facilities, active commuting, risks of sitting too long*

## Text 1 — Problem–solution essay
> People today are much less physically active than in the past. Why is this the case, and what can be done to encourage people to exercise more?

The decline in **physical activity** has several causes. **First and foremost**, work has changed: millions of people now have **desk jobs** and **sit for long periods**. **On top of this**, **car dependence** means that many adults rarely make even short trips **on foot**, and in the evening they end up **slumped in front of the TV**, since shopping and entertainment are available **at the touch of a button**. Children are affected too, as busy parents drive them to school and screens have replaced **running around** outside. All of these trends **contribute to** **a sedentary lifestyle**, which **raises the risk of** **cardiovascular disease** and obesity.

**Dealing with** this problem requires action from schools, employers and local governments. Schools have **come under** pressure to reduce **PE** in favor of tested subjects, yet simple ideas can work. An elementary school principal in Stirling, Scotland, **came up with** the Daily Mile in 2012: students run or walk for fifteen minutes every day, and thousands of schools worldwide have since adopted it. For adults, employers could offer **standing desks** and help with commuting costs, while cities expand **bike-share programs** and keep **recreation centers** affordable. For people who already struggle to **cope with** long working hours, even **moderate exercise** such as walking to the bus stop makes a difference. **Taken together**, these measures would make exercise part of everyday life rather than a chore.

**Notes**
- **Topic vocabulary:** *physical activity*, *car dependence*, *a sedentary lifestyle*, *cardiovascular disease* — the first two and the last are uncountable; *a sedentary lifestyle* takes *a*.
- **Collocations:** *sit for long periods*, *raise the risk of sth* (NOT *rise*), *moderate exercise* — precise, examiner-friendly pairs.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *contribute to* + noun (= be one of the causes), *deal with* + problem, *cope with* + pressure/hours — *Dealing with this problem* works as a paragraph opener.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *come up with* an idea, *come under* pressure / criticism (formal) — *have come under pressure to* + infinitive.
- **Fixed expressions:** *on foot* (NOT *by foot*), *at the touch of a button*, *slumped in front of the TV* — the last is informal-neutral; acceptable once in an essay.
- **AmE terms:** *a bike-share program* (BrE *cycle hire scheme*), *a recreation center* (BrE *leisure centre*), *in favor of* (BrE *favour*).
- **Frames:** *First and foremost, …* / *On top of this, …* — a clear order of causes without *Firstly, Secondly*.
- **Frame:** *Taken together, these measures would …* — a natural concluding line for any solutions paragraph.
- **Specific example:** the Daily Mile, started in 2012 by an elementary school principal (UK: head teacher) in Stirling, Scotland, now used by thousands of schools.

## Text 2 — Letter (informal)
> You have joined a running club and are taking part in a charity race next month. Write a letter to a friend. In your letter, tell them how you started running, describe the race and the charity, and invite your friend to join you.

Hi Sam,

How are things? Sorry I've been quiet — you'll never guess what I've been up to. I've joined a running club!

It all started in January, when I finally **faced up to** the fact that I was completely **out of shape**. I got **out of breath** just climbing the stairs, so I joined a beginners' group at the **recreation center**. The first few weeks were tough, but I **stuck with it**, and now I can run 5K without stopping.

Next month, on Sunday the 14th, our club is **taking part in** a 10K **race** along the river to **raise money for** the children's hospice in town, which supports seriously ill young people and their families. It's a really friendly event — plenty of people walk some of it.

Why don't you join me? You don't need to be **a fitness fanatic**; we'd have a few weeks to **build up** your **stamina**. Come up on Saturday and I'll **pick you up** from the station. You can **stay over** — I can easily **put you up** — and my brother has offered to **drop us off** at the starting line.

Let me know what you think!

Talk soon,
Anna

**Notes**
- **Fixed expressions:** *be out of shape*, *out of breath*, *a fitness fanatic* — everyday fitness language that suits an informal letter.
- **Collocations:** *take part in a race*, *raise money for (a) charity* — the two key verbs for any charity-event letter.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *stick with it* (keep going; BrE also *stick at it*), *build up your stamina* (separable: *build it up*).
- **Phrasal verbs:** *face up to the fact that …* — admit something unpleasant; also works in essays (*governments must face up to the fact that …*).
- **Phrasal verbs:** *pick sb up* (from the station), *drop sb off* (at a place) — the object pronoun goes in the middle: *pick you up*, NOT *pick up you*.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *put sb up* (= give sb a bed) and *stay over* (= sleep at sb's home) — the host *puts you up*; the guest *stays over*.
- **Frames:** *You'll never guess what I've been up to.* / *It all started in …, when …* — lively openers for any informal letter with news.
- **Frame:** *Why don't you join me? You don't need to be …* — an invitation plus reassurance; *Let me know what you think!* and *Talk soon,* close it naturally.
