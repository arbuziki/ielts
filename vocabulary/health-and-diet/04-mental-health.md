# Mental Health

## Vocabulary

|---|---|---|---|

## Text 1 — Two-part question
> Why do so many young people today suffer from stress and anxiety? Who should be responsible for helping them?

Young people today face pressures that earlier generations rarely experienced. **Exam stress** is the most obvious: college admissions are now so competitive that many teenagers believe they need top grades in every subject, and those who **fall behind** even slightly fear that their future is ruined. Many also struggle to **live up to** the **unrealistic expectations** of parents who have already chosen a career for them. Social media makes matters worse, as teenagers try to **keep up with** friends whose online lives look flawless, which feeds **perfectionism** and **low mood**. **Not surprisingly**, many of them **suffer from anxiety** yet **suffer in silence**, because asking for help still feels shameful.

**As for** responsibility, I believe schools should play the leading role, with families supporting them. Teachers see students every day, so they are well placed to **spot the warning signs** of **depression** early. At the high school I attended, for instance, one psychologist was responsible for over a thousand students, and most of us did not know where her office was. Funding more trained staff would **bring down** wait times for **counseling**, so that problems are treated before they **hold back** a young person's education. Classes that encourage students to **talk openly about** their feelings would also **tackle the stigma around** mental illness and help them **build resilience**.

**Notes**
- **Topic vocabulary:** *exam stress*, *perfectionism*, *low mood*, *depression*, *counseling* — all uncountable; NOT *an exam stress*, NOT *a counseling* (BrE spelling *counselling*).
- **Collocations:** *suffer from anxiety* (NOT *suffer of*), *spot the warning signs of sth*, *unrealistic expectations of sb*.
- **Collocations:** *tackle the stigma around sth*, *talk openly about sth*, *build resilience* — the key "solution" phrases for any mental-health essay.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *live up to* + expectations, *fall behind* (in a subject), *keep up with* + people / change — three ways to describe pressure.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *bring down* + wait times / costs / rates (= reduce); *hold back* + progress / education / sb (= stop sb developing).
- **Fixed expression:** *suffer in silence* — contrasts neatly with *suffer from anxiety* in the same sentence.
- **Frames:** *Not surprisingly, …* introduces a predictable result; *As for responsibility, …* moves smoothly to the second question.
- **Frame:** *X is / are well placed to + infinitive* (= in a good position to) — useful whenever you argue who should be responsible.
- **Specific example:** a personal one (one school psychologist for over a thousand students) — when you have no reliable statistic, a short personal example is safer.

## Text 2 — Letter (informal)
> A friend has written to say they are feeling very stressed and overwhelmed in college and are thinking of dropping out. Write a letter to your friend. In your letter, show that you understand how they feel, share some practical advice, and suggest where they could get support.

Hi Tom,

Thanks for your letter — I'm really sorry things have been so hard. It sounds as if you've **been under** huge **pressure**: **a heavy workload**, **deadlines** you're struggling to **meet** and, on top of that, extra shifts at the coffee shop because rising rents have **driven up** your living costs. No wonder you're **at the end of your rope**.

Please don't **drop out** just yet. In my sophomore year I **bottled** everything **up** for months, and it was only when I **talked** it all **through** with someone at student services that I could **put things into perspective**. Your college almost certainly has a free **counseling** service, so book an appointment this week. Your professors may also be able to extend some deadlines.

In the meantime, take care of yourself. **Sleep in** on the weekend, and try to **work out at the gym** a couple of times a week — it really helps. And remember how **into** photography you **were** in high school? Maybe it's time to **get** back **into** it.

**Don't be so hard on yourself.** Take it **one step at a time**, and call me anytime.

Take care,
Kate

**Notes**
- **Collocations:** *be under (huge) pressure*, *a heavy workload* (NOT *a big workload*), *meet a deadline* (NOT *catch*).
- **Fixed expressions:** *be at the end of your rope* (BrE *tether*), *put things into perspective*, *one step at a time*, *don't be so hard on yourself* — warm, natural support language.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *bottle sth up* / *talk sth through* — opposites; the object goes in the middle when it is a pronoun: *talk it through*.
- **Phrasal verb:** *drop out (of college)* — echoes the task so the reader sees you have answered it.
- **Phrasal verb:** *drive up* + costs / prices / rents (= make rise) — neutral, so it also works in essays on housing or tourism.
- **Informal chunks:** *sleep in* (= stay in bed late; BrE *have a lie-in*), *work out at the gym* (= exercise) — friendly advice without sounding like a doctor.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *be into sth* (= like a lot) vs *get into sth* (= start to like) — *be into* is a state, *get into* is a change.
- **Frames:** *It sounds as if you've …* / *No wonder you're …* — show understanding; *Please don't … just yet* gives gentle advice; *Take care,* is a warm informal sign-off.
