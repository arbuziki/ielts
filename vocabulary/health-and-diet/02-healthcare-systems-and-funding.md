# Healthcare Systems & Funding
*Health & Diet · free public healthcare vs private care, waiting lists, health insurance, prevention vs treatment, spending priorities, unequal access to care*

## Vocabulary

### Key terms
| Chunk | Meaning | Example | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| universal healthcare | a system that gives medical care to every citizen | Were the US to adopt **universal healthcare**, millions of families would no longer fear going bankrupt after a serious illness. | U · neutral · also *universal health coverage* · AmE also spelled *health care* · Grammar: inversion conditional |
| private healthcare | medical care that patients or their insurers pay for | The longer waiting lists grow, the more patients turn to **private healthcare** for routine operations. | U · neutral · NOT *a private healthcare* · Grammar: the more…, the more… |
| health insurance | a plan that pays your medical costs in return for regular payments | Workers whose employers do not offer **health insurance** often go without regular checkups and screenings. | U · neutral · *have / buy / get health insurance* · *a health insurance plan* · Grammar: relative: whose / whereby / in which |

| preventive medicine | care that aims to keep people from getting sick | **Preventive medicine** is widely thought to save health systems money by reducing the number of hospital admissions. | U · neutral · BrE also: *preventative* · Grammar: impersonal passive |
| a family doctor | a doctor who treats general health problems in the community | Rural clinics are so short of staff that it can take weeks to get an appointment with **a family doctor**. | C · neutral · formal: *a primary care physician (PCP)* · BrE: *a GP* · Grammar: so/such… that |
| medical bills | money you must pay for treatment | Many uninsured families cannot pay their **medical bills**, which forces some of them into bankruptcy. | C, plural · neutral · *run up / pay medical bills* · Grammar: sentential relative |

## Text 1 — Discuss both views
> Some people think that healthcare should be free for everyone and paid for by the government. Others believe individuals should pay for their own medical treatment. Discuss both views and give your opinion.

Those who believe individuals should pay for their own treatment **point out** that public systems are often under enormous pressure. When a government has to **cut back on** spending, **long wait times** follow, and frustrated patients end up paying for **private healthcare** anyway. If people were responsible for their own **health insurance**, the argument goes, they would also have a stronger reason to look after themselves. **Nevertheless**, I believe that basic healthcare should be **free at the point of use** and paid for with **taxpayer money**. Illness does not choose its victims according to income, and a system based on payment means that poorer people **put off** **seeking medical treatment** until a minor problem becomes serious. Britain's National Health Service, founded in 1948, **set out** to provide care **regardless of ability to pay**, and public support for that principle remains strong today. In the United States, by contrast, many people have to **draw on** their savings or borrow money to cover **medical bills**, and those without insurance may face huge costs for routine care. **This is not to say that** private care has no place: people who want to avoid **a waiting list** should be free to **shell out** for it. However, I would **call on** every government to guarantee **universal healthcare** first, because good health should not depend on the size of a person's bank account.

**Notes**
- **Topic vocabulary:** *universal healthcare*, *private healthcare*, *health insurance* — all uncountable; NOT *a health insurance*.
- **Collocations:** *seek medical treatment* (formal), *long wait times* (BrE *waiting times*), *taxpayer money* (BrE usually *taxpayers' money*).
- **Phrasal verbs:** *point out* + *that*-clause (report the other side's view), *cut back on spending*, *draw on savings / resources / experience*.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *set out to* + infinitive (= intend from the start), *call on sb to do sth* (= formally ask sb to act; compare *call for* + noun), *put off* + *-ing*.
- **Fixed expressions:** *free at the point of use*, *regardless of ability to pay* — the two standard phrases for public healthcare; learn them without articles.
- **Informal contrast:** *shell out for sth* is informal, used here for color; in a formal letter prefer *pay for*.
- **Frames:** *the argument goes* (= according to this view) lets you report an opinion you do not share; *Nevertheless, I believe …* turns to your own view.
- **Frame:** *This is not to say that X has no place: …* — a concession that keeps your position clear; works for cars, exams, private schools.
- **Specific example:** the NHS (founded 1948) vs the US, where many people use savings or loans to pay for treatment.

## Text 2 — Essay paragraph — Spend more of the health budget on prevention, not only treatment
> Some people think governments should spend more money on preventing illness than on treating people who are already ill. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

A larger share of **the health budget**, I would argue, should go to prevention rather than to treatment alone. Many of the conditions that fill hospital beds today **stem from** smoking, poor diet and inactivity, and **chronic conditions** such as type 2 diabetes **give rise to** years of expensive care. Much of this suffering could be avoided if illnesses were **detected at an early stage**: **screening** programs find cancers before they spread, while **vaccination** protects not only individuals but whole communities. The North Karelia Project in Finland shows what is possible. Launched in 1972 in a region with one of the world's highest rates of heart disease, it worked with local health services, food producers and the media to persuade people to smoke less and eat less salt and fat, and deaths from heart disease fell dramatically over the following decades. Prevention also keeps people out of the hospital, which **results in** shorter waiting lists for everyone else. The difficulty **arises from** the political calendar: the benefits of prevention appear years later, whereas a new hospital wing makes headlines at once. Yet **an ounce of prevention is worth a pound of cure**, and spending accordingly would **lead to** a healthier population and a far less **overburdened health system**. Every dollar spent on **public health** is, **in effect**, **money well spent**.

**Notes**
- **Topic vocabulary:** *screening*, *vaccination*, *public health* — the three pillars of prevention; *screening* and *public health* are uncountable.
- **Collocations:** *detect sth at an early stage* (NOT *in an early stage*), *an overburdened health system*, *the health budget*.
- **Cause verbs:** *stem from* + cause (formal) and *arise from* + cause — the problem is the subject: *Many conditions stem from smoking.*
- **Effect verbs:** *give rise to* + problem (formal), *result in* + effect, *lead to* + effect — the cause is the subject. Do not mix up the two directions.
- **Fixed expressions:** *an ounce of prevention is worth a pound of cure* (AmE; BrE *prevention is better than cure*), *money well spent* — strong, natural endings for any spending argument.
- **Frame:** *A larger share of X, I would argue, should go to Y rather than to Z alone* — *spend money on* sth, NOT *spend for*.
- **Frame:** *The difficulty arises from …: X, whereas Y* — explains why a sensible policy is not adopted; works for climate or transportation topics.
- **Frame:** *X is, in effect, Y* — sums up the practical meaning of your point in one clause; AmE *out of the hospital* (BrE *out of hospital*).
- **Specific example:** Finland's North Karelia Project (1972), a community campaign after which heart-disease deaths fell dramatically.
