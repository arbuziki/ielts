# Diet, Obesity & Fast Food
*Health & Diet · rising obesity, fast and processed food, sugar taxes, food labeling, school lunches, cooking skills, personal choice vs government intervention*

## Text 1 — Opinion essay
> Some people think governments should introduce a tax on sugary drinks and fast food to fight obesity. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

I largely agree that taxing sugary drinks is a sensible way to **tackle obesity**, although I am less convinced that the same approach would work for fast food. The main argument **put forward** against such a tax is that diet is **a matter of personal choice**, so **government intervention** unfairly punishes people who drink cola only occasionally. **Admittedly**, nobody **looks forward to** paying more at the checkout, and **a regressive tax** of this kind may **hit the poorest hardest**. The real aim, **however**, is not to raise money but to change what is on the shelves. When the UK announced its Soft Drinks Industry Levy in 2016, many manufacturers **reformulated their products** before it went into effect in 2018, so consumers took in less sugar without paying more. Despite pressure from the beverage industry, the government refused to **call off** the levy, and it **sent a clear message** that **soda** **is high in sugar** yet offers only **empty calories**. Fast food is a harder case. A burger meal can **account for** half of an adult's daily calories, but taxing whole meals would be complicated and might simply push families toward cheap **processed food** from supermarkets. **On balance**, a targeted tax on sugary drinks can **bring about** real change, provided it forms part of a wider strategy rather than being the only measure.

**Notes**
- **Topic vocabulary:** *obesity*, *government intervention*, *processed food* — uncountable: no *a*, no plural. *Soda* is usually uncountable too (*a soda* = one can); BrE: *fizzy drinks*.
- **Collocations:** *tackle obesity*, *reformulate products*, *be high in sugar* — exact verb–noun and adjective–preposition pairs; NOT *rich of sugar*.
- **Collocation:** *a regressive tax* + *hit the poorest hardest* — the standard objection to any consumption tax (gas, alcohol, plastic bags).
- **Phrasal verbs:** *put forward an argument* (formal), *call off sth* (= cancel), *bring about change* (= cause), *account for* + share/amount (= make up).
- **Phrasal verb:** *look forward to* + *-ing*: *nobody looks forward to paying more* — the *to* is a preposition, so NOT *to pay*.
- **Fixed expressions:** *a matter of personal choice*, *send a clear message that …* — both reusable in essays on smoking, alcohol or helmets.
- **Frames:** *Admittedly, … The real aim, however, is …* — concede a point, then return to your argument; *however* in mid-sentence avoids repeating *However,* at the start.
- **Frame:** *On balance, X can …, provided it forms part of a wider strategy* — a measured conclusion for any policy question.
- **Specific example:** the UK's Soft Drinks Industry Levy (announced 2016, in effect 2018) led many companies to cut sugar before it began. AmE *went into effect* = BrE *came into force*.

## Text 2 — Essay paragraph — Teaching cooking and making healthy food cheaper works better than taxing unhealthy food
> Some people think governments should introduce a tax on sugary drinks and fast food to fight obesity. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

In the long run, teaching people how to eat well and making healthy food cheaper would achieve more than taxing unhealthy food. A tax can make a can of cola more expensive, but it cannot teach a teenager to **cook a meal from scratch**; without that skill, young adults tend to **live on** **frozen dinners** or **eat out** several times a week. Japan offers a useful model. Since 2005 a national law has promoted food education, and in most public elementary schools students serve freshly prepared lunches to their classmates, learning what **a balanced diet** looks like. Diet is not the only factor, but Japan has one of the lowest **obesity rates** in the developed world. Other countries could **phase in** required cooking classes over several years while **phasing out** vending machines that sell chips and candy in schools. Price matters too. **Fresh produce** often costs more than **processed food**, so low-income families understandably **opt for** whatever is cheapest, and children **fill up on** snacks of little **nutritional value**. If governments **subsidized fruit and vegetables**, **making healthier choices** would no longer be a luxury. I would not **rule out** taxes altogether, but policymakers should first **carry out** research into what families actually buy and why. **After all**, there is **no silver bullet**: lasting change starts in the kitchen, not at the checkout.

**Notes**
- **Topic vocabulary:** *frozen dinners*, *fresh produce*, *nutritional value* — *produce* is uncountable (NOT *produces*); *frozen dinners* is usually plural (BrE: *ready meals*).
- **Collocations:** *subsidize fruit and vegetables* (BrE *subsidise*), *make healthier choices*, *obesity rates* — use *make* with *choices*, never *do*.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *phase in* / *phase out* + noun (= introduce / remove gradually) — a perfect pair for any reform essay.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *opt for* + noun (= choose), *rule out* + noun (= exclude as a possibility), *carry out research* (NOT *make research*; also *conduct research*).
- **Phrasal verbs:** *live on* + food, *fill up on* + food, *eat out* — natural everyday verbs that show range without being slang.
- **Fixed expressions:** *cook a meal from scratch*, *there is no silver bullet* — the second fits any problem-solution essay.
- **Frame:** *I would not rule out X altogether, but …* — a polite way to keep the other side's solution while preferring your own.
- **Frame:** *After all, …* introduces a final reason the reader should already accept; ends the paragraph with a memorable contrast (*in the kitchen, not at the checkout*).
- **Specific example:** Japan's food-education law (2005) and school lunches served by students; low obesity is stated carefully (*not the only factor*).
