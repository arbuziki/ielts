## Text 1 — Problem and solution
> In many cities, house prices and rents have risen so much that young people can no longer afford to live there. What are the causes of this problem, and what measures could be taken to solve it?

In cities such as New York, Sydney and Toronto, rent can now take **the lion's share of** a young person's income, and **getting into the housing market** seems like an impossible dream. **To a large extent**, the problem stems from a lack of supply. For decades, cities have failed to **build** enough **new homes** to **meet demand**, while strict zoning rules and **rising land values** have made each new apartment more expensive. Prices have also been **pushed up** by investors, who **snap up** condos as a safe place for their money, and by the spread of **short-term rentals**, which take homes off the long-term rental market altogether. As a result, **soaring rents** **eat into** young workers' salaries, and many are **priced out of** the neighborhoods they grew up in.

Solving this requires action on several fronts. **In the short term**, governments can **crack down on** illegal vacation rentals and cap annual rent increases, which protects existing tenants **to some extent**. **In the long run**, however, there is no substitute for building far more **affordable housing**. Vienna is **a case in point**: the city has invested in **public housing** for a century, and today a large share of its residents rent city-built or subsidized apartments at reasonable prices. If other cities followed this model, a secure home would no longer depend on how much money someone's parents have.

**Notes**
- **Topic vocabulary:**
  - *affordable housing / public housing* (U) — no article and no plural; say "more affordable housing", "far more homes".
  - *short-term rentals* (C) — the precise term for vacation rentals on booking apps (BrE: short-term lets).
- **Collocations:**
  - *build new homes to meet demand*; *soaring rents*; *rising land values* — the core "causes" paragraph in three chunks.
- **Phrasal verbs:**
  - *push up + prices*; *snap up + condos*; *eat into + salaries*; *be priced out of + place*.
  - *crack down on + illegal rentals / tax evasion / speeding* — reusable in crime and law topics.
- **Fixed expressions & frames:**
  - *getting into the housing market seems like an impossible dream* — a vivid way to open a housing essay (BrE: getting on the property ladder).
  - *To a large extent, the problem stems from + noun.* — a clean way to start the causes section.
  - *In the short term, … which helps / protects sb to some extent. In the long run, however, …* — a ready-made structure for any solutions paragraph.
  - *X is a case in point: …* — introduce your best example straight after the claim.

## Text 2 — Essay paragraph — Build upward, not outward
> Some people think growing cities should expand into the surrounding countryside. Others believe they should build upward on land that has already been developed. Argue for building upward.

Instead of pushing **the urban growth boundary** outward into farmland, cities should build upward on land that has already been developed. **As a rule**, **urban sprawl** is the most expensive way to house people: every new subdivision on the edge of town needs new roads, schools and sewers, and its residents usually depend on cars because buses are not worth running at such low densities. **High-density housing** on **brownfield sites**, however, makes use of infrastructure that already exists, and **it stands to reason that** a neighborhood with more people per square mile can support frequent buses, local stores and doctors' offices within walking distance. London's King's Cross shows what is possible: former railroad land that lay derelict for years has become **a mixed-use development** of apartments, offices, a university campus and public squares. Critics oppose taller buildings **on the grounds that** they will recreate the failed **high-rises** of the 1960s, but well-designed buildings of six to ten stories bear little resemblance to them. **With regard to** the countryside, fields that are built on are lost forever, while building upward helps to **protect green spaces** for future generations. **In light of** growing concern about climate change and food security, governments can no longer treat farmland as an unlimited resource. The sensible choice is to build up, not out.

**Notes**
- **Topic vocabulary:**
  - *the urban growth boundary* (C, "the" for a specific city's boundary) vs. *urban sprawl* (U, no article) — a typical article trap.
  - *brownfield sites* vs. *greenfield sites* — learn the pair.
- **Collocations:**
  - *high-density housing*; *a mixed-use development of A, B and C*; *protect green spaces for future generations*.
  - *bear little resemblance to + noun* — a precise way to reject a comparison.
- **Phrasal verbs:**
  - *build up, not out* / *push sth outward into + area* — short, memorable verb pair for the conclusion of any planning essay. AmE prefers *upward / outward / toward* without -s.
- **Fixed expressions & frames:**
  - *As a rule, X is the most expensive way to + verb* — general claim first, then reasons.
  - *It stands to reason that + clause* — use for a logical step, not a fact that needs proof.
  - *Critics oppose X on the grounds that + clause, but …* — the standard way to answer an objection.
  - *With regard to + noun, …* / *In light of + noun, …* (BrE: in the light of) — formal topic shifters; use one of each per essay, not more.
