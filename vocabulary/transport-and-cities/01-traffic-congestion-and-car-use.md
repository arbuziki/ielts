## Text 1 — Opinion essay
> Traffic congestion is a growing problem in many cities. Some people think the best solution is to charge drivers a fee to enter downtown. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

I largely agree that charging drivers to enter downtown is the most effective way to **ease congestion**, provided that the money is used to improve the alternatives. The argument **boils down to** price: while road space is free, people treat it as unlimited, and **heavy traffic** becomes a fact of life during **rush hour**. **Congestion pricing** puts a price on that space and makes drivers **think twice** about short trips they could make on foot or by bus. London, which introduced its congestion charge in 2003, saw a clear fall in traffic inside the zone in the early years, and the program still **raises revenue** that must be spent on transportation. Opponents tend to **play down** the cost of doing nothing. When streets are **at a standstill**, the damage **spills over into** other areas of life: deliveries arrive late, ambulances lose valuable minutes and residents breathe in **exhaust fumes**. Charging can also **head off** worse problems in cities where **car ownership** is still rising fast. Admittedly, a fee hits low-income drivers hardest, so governments should **lay down** clear rules that **earmark** the income for cheaper buses, **park-and-ride lots** and safe **bike lanes**. With these safeguards in place, a charge is not just one option among many but the right place to start.

**Notes**
- **Topic vocabulary:**
  - *congestion pricing / car ownership* — both uncountable, so no article. (BrE: *a congestion charge*, countable.)
  - *rush hour* — in AmE usually no article: "during rush hour"; reuse in any topic about cities, work or stress.
- **Collocations:**
  - *ease congestion, heavy traffic, raise revenue* — safe, examiner-friendly partners (NOT *decrease traffic jams*, NOT *strong traffic*).
  - *earmark the income for + noun* — reuse for any tax: "earmark the money for schools" (BrE: ring-fence).
- **Phrasal verbs:**
  - *boil down to + noun* — "The argument boils down to price / cost / trust."
  - *play down + the cost / risks of + noun*; *head off + problems / a crisis*; *lay down + clear rules*.
  - *spill over into + other areas of life* — works for crime, stress, unemployment.
- **Fixed expressions & frames:**
  - *make sb think twice about + noun / -ing* — the classic way to describe any deterrent (fines, taxes, prices).
  - *Admittedly, …, so …* — concede a weakness, then turn it into a condition.

## Text 2 — Essay paragraph — Building more roads only creates more traffic
> Some people say the answer to traffic congestion is to build more and wider roads. Explain why this approach does not work.

Building more and wider roads may look like the obvious answer to congestion, but it rarely works for long. The reason is **induced demand**: when new road space appears, trips become quicker for a while, so people who previously took the train or traveled at quieter times start driving instead, and the extra lanes soon **fill up** again. Houston's Katy Freeway illustrates this well. It was **widened** to more than twenty lanes in places in the late 2000s at a cost of billions of dollars, yet within a few years rush-hour trips were taking longer than before. Despite such evidence, politicians often **bring forward** road projects ahead of elections because they look decisive. These projects can also do real harm: a new urban freeway may leave whole neighborhoods **cut off** from downtown, while people who **live on** low incomes and do not own a car gain nothing at all. In my view, whether a city escapes **gridlock** **hinges on** managing demand rather than adding capacity. Planners should therefore **weed out** projects that merely move **the bottleneck** a few miles down the road and spend the money on **a road network** that gives priority to buses and bikes. Otherwise, road-building simply feeds **a vicious cycle** of more lanes, more cars and more congestion.

**Notes**
- **Topic vocabulary:**
  - *induced demand* — the key academic term; define it in a colon clause, as here, the first time you use it.
  - *gridlock / the bottleneck* — uncountable vs. countable: "escape gridlock", "move the bottleneck".
- **Collocations:**
  - *widen a road / be widened to + number + lanes*; *a road network that gives priority to + noun*.
- **Phrasal verbs:**
  - *fill up* (intransitive) — "The new lanes soon filled up again."
  - *hinge on + -ing / wh-clause* — "Success hinges on managing demand."
  - *bring forward + projects / plans*; *weed out + projects / proposals that …*.
  - *be cut off from + place*; *live on + low incomes* — two ways to describe disadvantaged groups.
- **Fixed expressions & frames:**
  - *X illustrates this well.* — a short, neutral way to introduce an example (instead of "A case in point is…").
  - *feed a vicious cycle of A, B and C* — a strong closing frame for any self-reinforcing problem.
