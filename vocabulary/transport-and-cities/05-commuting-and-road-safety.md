## Text 1 — Two-part question
> Many people now spend more than two hours a day traveling to and from work. Why is this happening, and what effects does it have on individuals and society?

The main reason for such long trips is the price of housing. **First and foremost**, jobs are concentrated downtown, where nurses, teachers and retail workers can rarely afford to live, so they move to **bedroom communities** and travel back in every day. Transportation has not kept pace with this shift: suburban trains are slow, and drivers lose further time sitting in traffic at **peak times**.

The cost to individuals is considerable. Take a hypothetical nurse whose trip is an hour **door to door** each way: two hours a day adds up to around 450 hours a year, more than eleven 40-hour workweeks. That time is **taken up** by travel instead of sleep, exercise or family, and over the years it **takes a toll on** physical and mental health. **On the whole**, people with **a grueling commute** report a lower **quality of life**, and exhausted drivers are more likely to **lose concentration** on the way home. Society pays as well. Commuters who are **worn out** are less productive at work; **by the same token**, they have less energy for volunteering or local events, so communities suffer. **On the other hand**, **remote work** and **flexible hours** have given some employees a way out. **All things considered**, though, long commutes are a symptom of housing policy, and they will only shrink when people can live near where they work.

**Notes**
- **Topic vocabulary:**
  - *bedroom communities / the exurbs* — use them to explain *why*; avoid repeating "people live far from work".
  - *remote work* (U) — no article; "the rise of remote work".
- **Collocations:**
  - *a grueling commute*; *quality of life* (U); *lose concentration*; *at peak times*.
  - *take a toll on + health / relationships* — reusable in work, technology and stress topics.
- **Phrasal verbs:**
  - *time is taken up by + noun* — passive frame for wasted time.
  - *be worn out* — neutral enough for an essay when used as an adjective.
- **Fixed expressions & frames:**
  - *Take a hypothetical + person: …* — an honest way to give a specific example without inventing statistics; do the arithmetic yourself.
  - *First and foremost, …* (first cause) / *On the whole, …* (generalization) / *All things considered, …* (conclusion).
  - *A; by the same token, B* — B follows the same logic as A; *On the other hand, …* introduces the opposite side.

## Text 2 — Letter (formal)
> There is a busy road near your home that many children cross on their way to school, and there have been several near-accidents. Write a letter to the city council. In your letter: describe the road; explain the dangers; suggest what could be done to make it safer.

Dear Sir or Madam,

I am writing to draw your attention to the dangerous conditions on Mill Road, outside Greenfield Elementary School.

Mill Road is a wide, straight road with a 30 mph **speed limit**, but there is no **crosswalk** between the school entrance and the bus stop across the street. Drivers do not **slow down** near the school. **On the contrary**, many use the road as a shortcut and **exceed the speed limit** to beat the light at the **intersection**. Oak Avenue, **by contrast**, has **speed bumps** and a crossing guard, and accidents there are rare.

In the past month, I have witnessed three **near misses**, including one in which a boy was almost **run over** by a van **pulling out** of a side street. I appreciate that the city's budget is limited. **Even so**, it is **only a matter of time before** a child is seriously injured.

I would suggest installing a marked crosswalk and **traffic-calming measures** near the school. **That said**, these will take time, so a temporary crossing guard would be a sensible first step. Some drivers may object to the delays. **Be that as it may**, children's safety must come first.

Sincerely,
Daniel Kovalenko

**Notes**
- **Topic vocabulary:**
  - *a crosswalk / speed bumps / near misses / the intersection* — concrete nouns make a complaint credible; check each article. *A crossing guard* (BrE: a lollipop lady / crossing patrol) is the adult who helps children cross.
- **Collocations:**
  - *exceed the speed limit*; *traffic-calming measures* — formal register for letters to authorities.
- **Phrasal verbs:**
  - *slow down*; *be (almost) run over by + vehicle*; *pull out of + a side street* — describe an incident precisely.
- **Fixed expressions & frames:**
  - *I am writing to draw your attention to + noun* — a formal opening for any problem letter.
  - *X do not + verb. On the contrary, they + verb.* — use only after a negative statement.
  - *A, by contrast, …* — compare two places or groups.
  - *Even so, …* / *That said, …* / *Be that as it may, …* — three ways to concede and still push your point; use each only once.
  - *Dear Sir or Madam, … Sincerely,* — the standard AmE formal frame (BrE: Yours faithfully).
