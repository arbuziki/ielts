## Vocabulary

## Text 1 — Causes + positive or negative (two-part question)
> In many countries, extended families no longer live together, and family members see each other less often than in the past. Why has this happened? Is it a positive or negative development?

**The main reason why** **extended families** no longer share a home **is that** work now draws people away from where they grew up. Young adults **move away** to study or to find better-paid jobs, and greater **job mobility** means they may change cities several times. **Urbanization** has made this pattern especially visible in China, where hundreds of millions of rural workers have moved to cities since the 1980s, often leaving their children behind to be **brought up** by grandparents in the villages. Housing also plays a part, since **a cramped apartment** cannot fit three generations, and many couples simply value privacy more than their parents did.

In my view, this development is largely negative. When **family gatherings** become **few and far between**, children lose the chance to learn from their grandparents, while older relatives lose **emotional support** just when they need it most. That said, **it is not so much** distance **as** a lack of effort **that** weakens relationships. **There is no reason why** relatives who **live far apart** **should not** stay close, given cheap video calls and budget flights. Equally, **there is no point in** idealizing the past, when crowded homes often caused conflict. **It is essential**, therefore, **that** each generation **make** a deliberate effort to **strengthen family ties**, whether through weekly calls or regular visits.

**Notes**
- **Topic vocabulary:** *extended families* (C, plural here), *urbanization* (U) — the key cause/effect pair for any family-change question.
- **Collocations:** *job mobility* (U), *a cramped apartment* (C), *emotional support* (U) — three reasons and effects you can reuse in housing or migration essays.
- **Collocations:** *strengthen family ties* — ideal for a final solution sentence; *ties* is always plural here.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *move away* (from home), *bring sb up* — neutral, essay-safe.
- **Idiom:** *few and far between* — neutral enough for Task 2; use after a verb (*have become few and far between*).
- **Frame — The main reason why + clause + is that + clause:** opens a cause paragraph clearly; NOT *the reason is because*.
- **Frame — It is not so much X as Y (that…):** corrects a common assumption (*not so much the cost as the time…*).
- **Frames — There is no reason why + subject + should not…; There is no point in + -ing:** a quick way to reject an argument.
- **Frame — It is essential that + subject + base verb:** *each generation make* (subjunctive, no *-s*) — formal recommendation in any conclusion; the subjunctive is standard in AmE.

## Text 2 — Letter (informal)
> Your grandparents are celebrating a special wedding anniversary and your family is holding a big party. Write a letter to a friend. In your letter: invite your friend to the party; tell them about the event and the people who will be there; explain how to get there.

Hi Sam,

How are you? I hope you've **settled into** your new apartment – send me some photos!

I'm writing with some exciting news. My grandparents are celebrating their **golden wedding anniversary** next month, and we're throwing them a big party on Saturday, June 14. I'd love for you to come! It'll be a real **family reunion** – my whole **extended family** is **getting together**, including cousins from Canada I haven't seen in ten years. **Far from complaining** about the fuss, Grandpa has already written a speech, so be ready for some long stories!

You'll finally meet my Aunt Rosa, who's **the black sheep of the family** and the funniest person I know. My little sister will be there too – she's been studying for finals for weeks, so a party should **cheer** her **up**.

The party's at the Riverside Hotel, a ten-minute walk from the train station. If you're driving, turn left just after the bridge – there's a free parking lot behind the hotel. Why don't you stay at our place afterward? We can **catch up** properly and **take it easy** on Sunday – you need to **wind down** after all that unpacking.

Let me know if you can make it!

Talk soon,
Anna

**Notes**
- **Topic vocabulary:** *a golden wedding anniversary* (50 years), *a family reunion*, *my extended family* — precise nouns that make an invitation letter sound natural.
- **Phrasal verbs:** *get together* (meet as a group), *catch up* (exchange news) — the two most useful informal-letter verbs for meeting people.
- **Idiom:** *the black sheep of the family* — light humor suits an informal letter; avoid it in essays.
- **Phrasal verbs — settle into sth / settle in:** *I hope you've settled into your new apartment* — a friendly opening for any letter to someone who has moved.
- **Phrasal verbs — cheer sb up:** separable, *cheer her up*; use for any good news or treat.
- **Phrasal verb + fixed expression — wind down; take it easy:** relaxing after work or illness — great for closing invitations (*you can take it easy…*).
- **Letter conventions (AmE):** *Hi Sam,* to open and *Talk soon,* / *Best,* / *Take care,* to close; dates as *Saturday, June 14*.
- **Frame — Far from + -ing, + clause:** *Far from complaining…, Grandpa has already written a speech* — shows the opposite of what you might expect; formal, but adds wit even in a chatty letter.
