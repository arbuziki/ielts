## Text 1 — Opinion (to what extent do you agree?)
> Some people believe that all parents should be required to attend parenting classes before or after their child is born. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

**Appealing as it may seem**, making **parenting** classes mandatory would, in my opinion, go too far, although I strongly support offering them free of charge to everyone.

Supporters of the proposal have a point. **Raising a child** is one of the most demanding tasks an adult can take on, yet most **first-time parents** receive no training and learn **by trial and error**. A good course can show them how to **set boundaries**, survive **sleepless nights** and recognize which kinds of behavior in a **toddler** are perfectly normal. **With** more young couples **living** far from their own parents, such guidance is **all the more valuable** because there is often no grandmother nearby to offer it.

**Much as** I **agree** with the aim, however, forcing parents to attend would be impractical and unfair. Such a rule would be almost impossible to enforce, **not least because** no government could reasonably fine a mother for missing a session while caring for **a newborn**. It would also suggest that the state, rather than the family, should decide which **parenting style** is acceptable.

A better model already exists in the UK, where the National Health Service offers free **prenatal classes** but leaves the choice to expectant parents. Voluntary, high-quality courses on **child development**, available both before and after the birth, would reach the parents who want help without treating everyone as a potential failure.

**Notes**
- **Topic vocabulary:** *parenting* (U), *a parenting style* (C), *prenatal classes* (C; BrE: *antenatal classes* — the term you will hear in British sources) — exact terms instead of "lessons for parents".
- **Topic vocabulary:** *a toddler*, *a newborn* (both C) — specific nouns sound more precise than "a small child".
- **Collocations:** *raise a child*, *set boundaries*, *sleepless nights*, *first-time parents*, *child development* — the building blocks of any parenting essay.
- **Fixed expression:** *by trial and error* (no articles) — fits learning a language, a job or a skill too.
- **Frame — Adj + as it may seem, + clause:** *Appealing as it may seem, …* — a concession opening that immediately shows your position.
- **Frame — With + noun + -ing, + clause:** *With more young couples living far from…* — packs a background trend into a short phrase.
- **Frame — all the more + adj + because…:** *all the more valuable because…* — strengthens a point you have already made.
- **Frame — Much as + subject + verb, + clause:** *Much as I agree with the aim, …* (= although) — polite disagreement.
- **Frame — …, not least because + clause:** introduces the most important of several reasons.

## Text 2 — Letter (informal)
> A close friend has just had their first baby. Write a letter to your friend. In your letter: congratulate your friend; share some advice from your own experience or from people you know; offer to help in a practical way.

Dear Tom,

Congratulations to you and Mia on the arrival of little Leo! Your photo made my day – he's adorable, and clearly **a chip off the old block**.

I know you've **got your hands full**, so I'll keep this short. The best advice I got when Ella was born was to sleep whenever the baby sleeps and forget about the housework. **To expect** a real routine in the first few weeks **would be** unrealistic – most babies take a while to **settle down** at night, so be ready for some **sleepless nights**. My sister always says **it takes a village to raise a child**, so don't be afraid to ask for help. And remember, **there's no such thing as a perfect parent** – it's **a steep learning curve** for everyone.

Now for something practical. I'm free most evenings next week, so how about I cook a few meals for your freezer and walk the dog so you two can rest? Just **drop me a line** or **give me a call** whenever works for you.

**Keep me posted** on how you're all doing, and let's **keep in touch** – I'll come by to meet Leo as soon as you're ready for visitors.

Take care,
Kate

**Notes**
- **Idioms:** *a chip off the old block*, *have your hands full* (also *have got your hands full*), *a steep learning curve* — warm, natural informal-letter language; *a steep learning curve* also works in essays.
- **Phrasal verb + collocation:** *settle down* (a baby falling asleep), *sleepless nights* — exactly what new parents talk about.
- **Fixed expressions:** *it takes a village to raise a child*; *there's no such thing as a perfect parent* — reuse the frame *there's no such thing as + noun* in any topic.
- **Fixed expressions — drop sb a line / give sb a call:** informal ways to say "write to / call me"; ideal for the end of any friendly letter (BrE also *give sb a ring*).
- **Fixed expressions — keep me posted (on + noun); keep in touch:** natural closing lines (*Keep me posted on the new job!*).
- **Letter conventions (AmE):** *Dear Tom,* or *Hi Tom,* to open; *Take care,* / *Best,* / *Talk soon,* to close.
- **Frame — To + verb + would be + adj:** *To expect a real routine… would be unrealistic* — formal, so in essays use it for a balanced judgment (*To ban cars entirely would be unrealistic*).
