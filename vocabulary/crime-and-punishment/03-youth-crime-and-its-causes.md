*Crime & Punishment · teenage offending, peer pressure, gangs, poverty, family breakdown, boredom, role models and early intervention*

## Text 1 — Problem and solution
> In many countries, the number of crimes committed by teenagers is rising. What are the causes of this, and what solutions can you suggest?

Rising youth crime has several connected causes. The first is economic. When local factories **shut down** or **lay off** workers, whole communities become **disadvantaged neighborhoods** with **a lack of opportunities**, and parents under financial pressure can provide less **parental supervision**. Cuts to **youth facilities** make the problem worse: teenagers with **time on their hands** tend to **hang around** on the streets, where it is easy to **fall in with** **the wrong crowd**. For a teenager who feels ignored at home or at school, **a gang** can offer **a sense of belonging** and respect, and **peer pressure** does the rest.

The most promising solution is **early intervention**. Rather than waiting until teenagers have a criminal record, local governments should fund mentors, sports teams and apprenticeships that **keep** young people **off the streets** and give them **role models** to **look up to**. Glasgow shows what this approach can achieve. In 2005, Scottish police **set up** a Violence Reduction Unit that treated **knife crime** as a public health problem, combining firm policing with jobs, mentoring and support for families. Violent crime in the city fell sharply over the following decade, and the idea **took off** elsewhere, with London launching its own unit in 2019. Other governments should **go ahead with** similar long-term programs, because it is far cheaper to **divert** young people **away from crime** than to deal with the consequences later.

**Notes**
- **Topic vocabulary:**
  - *disadvantaged neighborhoods*, *youth facilities* — both plural; "cuts to youth facilities" is a natural cause phrase. BrE: *deprived areas*.
  - *early intervention* (U) — the key word for any "solution" paragraph on social problems.
- **Collocations:**
  - *a lack of opportunities* — *a lack of* + plural or U noun, with no *the* after *of*: NOT "a lack of the opportunities".
  - *a sense of belonging* — explains *why* gangs attract teenagers; reusable for social media, sports, religion.
- **Phrasal verbs:**
  - *factories shut down* / *companies lay off workers* — the classic economic-decline pair (noun: *layoffs*).
  - *hang around* + *fall in with the wrong crowd* — a natural cause chain.
  - *set up a unit / program*, *an idea takes off*, *go ahead with a program* — the life of a project from start to success. In AmE, *scheme* often suggests a dishonest plan, so prefer *program* or *initiative*.
- **Fixed expressions & frames:**
  - *Rising X has several connected causes. The first is …* — a clean opening for any problem-solution essay.
  - *X shows what this approach can achieve.* — introduces a real example.
  - *it is far cheaper to X than to deal with the consequences later* — a strong closing argument for prevention.

## Text 2 — Letter (informal)
> A close friend has written to tell you that their teenage son has gotten into trouble with the police for shoplifting with a group of friends. Write a letter to your friend. In your letter: show sympathy; suggest why this might have happened; give some advice.

Dear Anna,

I'm so sorry to hear about Max. I can only imagine what you've been **going through**, but please don't blame yourself. Plenty of good kids **get into trouble** at that age, and most **grow out of** it quickly.

From what you said, it sounds like he's **fallen in with** **the wrong crowd** and the others **egged** him **on**. At fifteen, **peer pressure** is enormous, and **shoplifting** with his buddies was probably more about looking cool than wanting the stuff. It might also be **a cry for help** – he's found the move to a new school really hard, hasn't he?

My advice would be to stay calm and talk to him rather than yelling. Make sure he apologizes to the store owner himself. It might help to find something that **keeps** him **off the streets** after school. Our local boxing gym is amazing, and the coach is a great **role model**. Tom, who trains there, would be happy to **give** you **a hand** with the sign-up forms.

I'll **drop by** on Saturday afternoon, so you can **fill** me **in** on everything. And I **can't wait to** see Max beat Tom at chess again!

Take care,
Kate

**Notes**
- **Topic vocabulary:**
  - *shoplifting* (U) — no article; *go shoplifting*, *be caught shoplifting* (+ -ing).
  - *a cry for help* — shows empathy; a soft, natural way to suggest a deeper reason.
- **Collocations:**
  - *peer pressure is enormous* — simple, informal, but precise.
- **Phrasal verbs:**
  - *go through a hard time* — "what you've been going through" is the standard sympathy phrase.
  - *get into trouble*, *grow out of it*, *fall in with*, *egg sb on* — the informal story of teenage misbehavior. AmE past participle: *has gotten into trouble* (BrE: *has got*).
  - *give sb a hand with sth*, *drop by*, *fill sb in on sth* — friendly offers for the end of any informal letter.
- **Fixed expressions & frames:**
  - *I'm so sorry to hear about … I can only imagine what you've been going through.* — a ready-made sympathy opening.
  - *From what you said, it sounds like …* — a soft way to suggest a reason.
  - *My advice would be to … rather than …* — gentle, natural advice frame.
  - *I can't wait to + infinitive* — warm, positive close; sign off with *Take care,* / *Love,* / *Talk soon,* (BrE: *Lots of love,*).
