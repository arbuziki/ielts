## Vocabulary

### Key terms
| Chunk | Meaning | Example | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| surveillance | the careful watching of people, especially by the police or the state | Mass **surveillance** is said to prevent terrorist attacks, but critics question how often it actually does. | U · neutral · *mass surveillance*; NOT *a surveillance* · Grammar: impersonal passive |

## Text 1 — Advantages and disadvantages
> CCTV cameras are increasingly being installed in streets, stores and public transportation. Do the advantages of this development outweigh the disadvantages?

Cameras have become a normal part of city life, and in my view their benefits outweigh their drawbacks, provided that their use is clearly regulated.

The main advantage is that **CCTV** helps police **identify suspects** and **gather evidence** quickly. After the riots in England in 2011, footage from thousands of cameras **turned out** to be essential: officers used it to **track down** hundreds of people who had looted stores. Cameras that **monitor public spaces** such as subway stations and parking lots also discourage opportunistic offenders, and knowing that someone is **keeping an eye on** the street makes many people feel safer.

There are, admittedly, genuine drawbacks. Some research suggests that cameras merely **displace crime** to streets without them, while residents may develop **a false sense of security** and stop taking basic precautions. More seriously, the spread of **facial recognition** **raises concerns about** **civil liberties**: if every face in a crowd can be identified, ordinary citizens could **end up** living in **a surveillance state**.

However, these risks are a reason to regulate cameras, not to **do away with** them. Governments should **put** laws **in place** that limit who can access footage and require operators to **get rid of** recordings after a fixed period unless they are needed for an investigation. With such safeguards, CCTV remains **a double-edged sword**, but one that does considerably more good than harm.

**Notes**
- **Topic vocabulary:**
  - *CCTV* (U) — "on CCTV", "CCTV footage", "CCTV cameras"; never *a CCTV*. IELTS often uses this BrE term; in everyday AmE, say *security cameras*.
  - *civil liberties* (plural) / *a surveillance state* (C) — the key words for the privacy side of any technology essay.
- **Collocations:**
  - *identify suspects*, *gather evidence*, *monitor public spaces* — three verb + noun pairs that describe what cameras do.
  - *displace crime* — a precise, examiner-friendly counter-argument to "cameras reduce crime".
- **Phrasal verbs:**
  - *sth turned out to be essential* — reveals a result you only knew later.
  - *could end up living in …* — a natural way to describe an unwanted final result.
  - *do away with sth* (abolish) / *get rid of sth* (remove, delete) / *put laws in place* (introduce) — the vocabulary of policy change. BrE also: *bring in laws*.
- **Fixed expressions & frames:**
  - *There are, admittedly, genuine drawbacks.* — a mature concession sentence.
  - *These risks are a reason to regulate X, not to do away with it.* — a reusable rebuttal frame for technology topics.
  - *X remains a double-edged sword, but one that does considerably more good than harm.* — a balanced final verdict.

## Text 2 — Letter (formal)
> There has recently been a series of break-ins and car thefts in your neighborhood. Write a letter to the local police chief. In your letter: describe what has happened; explain how residents feel; request specific action.

Dear Chief Morgan,

I am writing on behalf of the residents of Elm Park Road to report a series of **break-ins** and car thefts and request your help.

Since early March, five homes have been burglarized and four cars stolen, mostly at night. Twice, neighbors **reported a crime** within minutes, yet officers did not arrive until the next morning. Although one doorbell camera **picked up** an image of **a suspect**, we do not know whether anyone has **looked into** this lead.

Residents are anxious about these slow **response times**, and several families have **cut down on** evenings out because they no longer feel safe leaving their homes empty. Many feel that our area **loses out** to downtown, which has a far more visible **police presence**.

I would therefore be grateful if you could **step up** nighttime patrols and ensure every report is **followed up on**. We plan to start **a neighborhood watch program** and would welcome advice on how it could **fit in** with your operations. While we appreciate the need to **keep** costs **down**, we hope residents will not **miss out on** the protection they deserve.

I look forward to hearing from you.

Sincerely,
Daniel Brooks

**Notes**
- **Topic vocabulary:**
  - *a series of break-ins* — *a series of* + plural noun usually takes a singular verb ("a series of break-ins **has** …").
  - *response times*, *a visible police presence* — precise terms instead of "the police are slow / never here".
- **Collocations:**
  - *report a crime (to the police)* — NOT *inform a crime*; *within minutes* adds urgency.
  - *homes have been burglarized* — AmE verb; BrE: *burgled*.
- **Phrasal verbs:**
  - *a camera picked up an image of a suspect*, *look into a lead*, *step up patrols*, *follow up on a report* — the language of police action.
  - *cut down on evenings out* — reusable for any change in habits (*cut down on sugar / car use*).
  - *our area loses out to downtown* / *miss out on protection* — polite ways to complain about unfair treatment.
  - *fit in with your operations*, *keep costs down* — show that you understand the other side's limits.
- **Fixed expressions & frames:**
  - *I am writing on behalf of … to report … and request your help.* — a complete formal opening in one sentence.
  - *I would therefore be grateful if you could …* — the standard polite request; no contractions.
  - *While we appreciate the need to …, we hope …* — a diplomatic concession before the final request.
  - *Dear Chief Morgan, … Sincerely,* — AmE formal letter with a named reader (BrE: *Yours sincerely,*).
