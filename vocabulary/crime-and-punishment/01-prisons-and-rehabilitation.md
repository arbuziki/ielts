# Prisons vs Rehabilitation
*Crime & Punishment · the purpose of prison, reoffending, overcrowding, education and training behind bars, and life after release*

## Vocabulary

### Key terms
| Chunk | Meaning | Example | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| a custodial sentence | a punishment that involves time in prison | Were judges to pass **a custodial sentence** only for violent crimes, prison overcrowding would ease considerably. | C · formal, mainly BrE legal term · opposite: *a non-custodial sentence*; a judge *passes* it · everyday AmE: *a prison sentence* / *jail time* · Grammar: inversion conditional |
| rehabilitation | helping offenders return to a normal, law-abiding life | Only by investing in **rehabilitation** can prisons hope to reduce crime in the long run. | U · neutral · NOT *a rehabilitation* · Grammar: negative inversion |
| deterrence | stopping crime by making people afraid of punishment | It is widely believed that long sentences reduce crime through **deterrence**, but the evidence is surprisingly weak. | U · formal · cf. *a deterrent* (C) = the thing that deters · Grammar: impersonal passive |
| reoffending | committing another crime after being punished | The longer ex-prisoners stay unemployed, the higher the risk of **reoffending** becomes. | U · neutral · formal synonym, very common in AmE: *recidivism* (U) · Grammar: the more…, the more… |
| a repeat offender | a person who commits crimes again and again | Given his long criminal record, the burglar should have been sentenced as **a repeat offender**, not as a first-timer. | C · neutral · = *a persistent offender* · Grammar: modal perfect |
| an ex-offender | someone who has completed a punishment for a crime | Much as employers claim to support second chances, few are actually willing to hire **an ex-offender**. | C · neutral · *an* before a vowel sound · AmE also: *a formerly incarcerated person* · Grammar: concession |
| an inmate | a person kept in a prison | Having spent fifteen years as **an inmate**, he found even a trip to the grocery store overwhelming after his release. | C · neutral · = *a prisoner* · Grammar: participle clause |
| the prison population | the total number of people in prison in a country | In many countries, **the prison population** has grown faster than the number of cells, which has led to severe overcrowding. | singular, always with *the* · neutral · Grammar: sentential relative |
| overcrowding | having too many people in too small a space | **Overcrowding** has become so severe in some prisons that inmates sleep on the floor and wait months for medical care. | U · neutral · *prison overcrowding*; NOT *an overcrowding* · Grammar: so/such… that |
| vocational training | training in practical skills for a particular job | What most prisoners need is **vocational training** in trades such as plumbing, cooking or carpentry. | U · neutral · NOT *a vocational training* · Grammar: cleft sentence |
| a criminal record | an official record of the crimes a person has committed | If he had not gotten **a criminal record** as a teenager, he would now be working as a teacher. | C · neutral · *have / get a criminal record* · Grammar: mixed conditional |
| parole | early release from prison on condition of good behavior | The board granted him **parole** two years early, thereby giving him the chance to reconnect with his children. | U · neutral · *on parole*, NOT *in parole* · Grammar: result clause with thereby/thus |
| a life sentence | the punishment of staying in prison for life or a very long time | No matter how much remorse he showed, the murderer was always going to receive **a life sentence**. | C · neutral · *receive / be given a life sentence* · Grammar: no matter / however |

### Collocations
| Chunk | Meaning | Example | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| serve a sentence | spend the time in prison that a court has ordered | A first-time offender should be allowed to **serve a** short **sentence** at home with an ankle monitor. | verb + noun · neutral · the judge *passes* (AmE also *hands down*) a sentence, the offender *serves* it · Grammar: modal passive |
| be sent to prison | be punished by a court with time in prison | It is high time fewer people **were sent to prison** for minor drug offenses. | passive · neutral · no article: NOT *sent to the prison* · Grammar: unreal past |
| be released from prison | be allowed to leave prison | Some states run a program whereby people who **are released from prison** receive temporary housing and job support. | passive · neutral · NOT *released of prison* · Grammar: relative: whose / whereby / in which |
| act as a deterrent (to sb) | discourage people from doing sth | Were prison sentences really to **act as a deterrent**, the countries with the harshest penalties would have the lowest crime rates. | deterrent = C, keep *a* · neutral · also *serve as a deterrent* · Grammar: inversion conditional |
| harsh sentences | very severe punishments | Not only do **harsh sentences** fill prisons, but they also do little to reduce reoffending. | adj + noun · neutral · opposite: *lenient sentences* · Grammar: negative inversion |
| a high reoffending rate | a large share of ex-prisoners committing new crimes | Short sentences are thought to contribute to **a high reoffending rate**, as they leave no time for rehabilitation. | C · neutral · *reduce / cut the reoffending rate* · AmE also: *a high recidivism rate* · Grammar: impersonal passive |
| reintegrate sb into society | help sb return to normal life in the community | The sooner ex-prisoners find stable housing and work, the easier it is to **reintegrate** them **into society**. | verb + object + into · formal · noun: *reintegration* (U) · Grammar: the more…, the more… |
| earn a certificate | get an official document showing you have completed a course or learned a skill | Even though they dropped out of high school, many inmates **earn** their first **certificates** behind bars. | verb + noun · neutral · AmE uses *earn* with *a certificate / a diploma / a degree* · BrE: *gain qualifications* · Grammar: concession |
| address the root causes of sth | deal with the basic reasons for a problem | What prisons rarely do is **address the root causes of** crime, such as addiction and poverty. | fixed collocation · formal · *the root causes* (plural, with *the*) · Grammar: cleft sentence |
| mental health support | help for people with psychological problems | Many suicides in prison might have been prevented with regular **mental health support**. | U · neutral · NOT *a mental health support* · Grammar: modal perfect |

## Text 1 — Discuss both views + opinion
> Some people believe that the best way to reduce crime is to give longer prison sentences. Others, however, think there are better ways of reducing crime. Discuss both views and give your opinion.

Supporters of longer sentences argue that keeping criminals **behind bars** protects the public and **acts as a deterrent** to anyone tempted to break the law. Politicians who want to look **tough on crime** promise **harsh sentences**, and it is true that a burglar who is **serving a** ten-year **sentence** cannot break into anyone's home.

However, I believe that longer terms do little to reduce crime in the long run. Almost every prisoner is eventually **released from prison**, and without help many **fall back into** their old habits, which turns the system into **a revolving door**. The United States **locks up** a larger share of its population than almost any other country, yet its murder rate remains several times higher than that of most Western European states. A better approach is to **set aside** money for **rehabilitation**, especially literacy classes and **vocational training**. Such programs cost money at first, but they **pay off**: each **ex-offender** who finds work is one fewer person **reoffending** **at the taxpayer's expense**. Long sentences also **pass** the problem **on** to the next generation. Children **brought up** by a parent who is in and out of jail often follow the same path, whereas a parent who has learned a trade can **hand down** useful skills.

On balance, long sentences may be justified for violent offenders, but for most crimes, investing in people is the smarter choice.

**Notes**
- **Topic vocabulary:**
  - *rehabilitation*, *reoffending* — both uncountable: "focus on rehabilitation", NOT "a rehabilitation".
  - *a revolving door* — a vivid but neutral image for people who keep returning; also works for hospitals or short-term jobs.
- **Collocations:**
  - *serve a ten-year sentence* — the length goes between *a* and *sentence*, hyphenated, no plural *-s* (*ten-year*, NOT *ten-years*).
  - *act as a deterrent to sb* — keep the article: *deterrent* is countable.
- **Phrasal verbs:**
  - *set aside money for sth* — reuse: *set aside land for housing*, *set aside time for studying*.
  - *sth pays off* — intransitive: "the investment pays off", NOT "pays off it".
  - *pass the problem on to sb* (transfer) vs *hand down skills (to children)* (from one generation to the next).
  - *children brought up by sb* — a reduced passive clause; reuse with any topic (*children brought up in cities*). AmE also says *raised by sb*.
- **Fixed expressions & frames:**
  - *X locks up a larger share of its population than almost any other country, yet …* — "comparison + yet" frame for using evidence against a view.
  - *each X who … is one fewer Y …* — a compact cost-benefit frame (*each cyclist is one fewer car on the road*).
  - *On balance, X may be justified for …, but for most …, Y is the smarter choice.* — a balanced conclusion.

## Text 2 — Essay paragraph — education and support inside prisons break the cycle of reoffending
> Write a paragraph arguing that education, vocational training and mental health support inside prisons are the most effective way to break the cycle of reoffending.

The most effective way to **break the cycle of reoffending** is to treat time in prison as an opportunity to learn rather than simply a punishment. A large proportion of **inmates** **dropped out of** school early, and many struggle with addiction or untreated mental illness, so **locking** them **up** without help does nothing to **address the root causes of** their offending. By contrast, inmates who **earn certificates**, **take up** a trade through **vocational training** and receive regular **mental health support** leave with a realistic chance of **turning their lives around**. Norway **stands out** as an example: prisons such as Halden are designed to resemble ordinary life as closely as possible, with workshops and classrooms, and the country has one of the lowest **reoffending** rates in Europe. Critics dismiss this model as soft, but the evidence **backs up** the opposite view, since **ex-offenders** with steady jobs are far less likely to commit new crimes. Politicians who **stand up for** such programs risk being accused of rewarding criminals, yet they are in fact protecting future victims. Education should therefore be a standard part of every sentence rather than an optional extra that prisoners can **opt out of**, because every former inmate who manages to **go straight** is one less burden on society.

**Notes**
- **Topic vocabulary:**
  - *inmates* (C) — a useful synonym to avoid repeating *prisoners*.
  - *vocational training* (U) — no article; pair it with *take up a trade through …*.
  - *a realistic chance of turning your life around* — *a chance of + -ing* is the safest pattern.
- **Collocations:**
  - *address the root causes of sth* — reusable for any social problem (*the root causes of obesity / homelessness*).
  - *earn certificates*, *receive mental health support* — natural verb partners; NOT *get a support*. BrE: *gain qualifications*.
- **Phrasal verbs:**
  - *drop out of school* (past: *dropped out of*) and *opt out of a program / an extra* — keep *of*.
  - *X stands out as an example* — an elegant way to introduce a real-world example.
  - *the evidence backs up the view that …* / *stand up for programs / rights* — useful for arguing.
- **Fixed expressions & frames:**
  - *The most effective way to X is to Y rather than Z.* — a strong topic sentence for any solution paragraph.
  - *Critics dismiss this as …, but the evidence backs up the opposite view, since …* — a ready-made rebuttal frame.
